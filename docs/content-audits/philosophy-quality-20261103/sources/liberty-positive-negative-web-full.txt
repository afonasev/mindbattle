L12: # Positive and Negative Liberty
L13:
L14: First published Thu Feb 27, 2003; substantive revision Sun Jun 28, 2026
L15: Negative liberty is the absence of obstacles, barriers or constraints. One has negative liberty to the extent that actions are available to one in this negative sense. Positive liberty is the possibility of acting—or the fact of acting—in such a way as to take control of one’s life and realize one’s fundamental purposes.
L16: While negative liberty is usually attributed to individual agents, positive liberty is sometimes attributed to collectivities, or to individuals considered primarily as members of given collectivities.
L17: The idea of distinguishing between a negative and a positive sense of the term ‘liberty’ goes back at least to Kant, and was examined and defended in depth by Isaiah Berlin in the 1950s and ’60s. Discussions about positive and negative liberty normally take place within the context of political and social philosophy. They are distinct from, though sometimes related to, philosophical discussions about 【8†free will】.
L18: Work on the nature of positive liberty often overlaps, however, with work on the nature of 【9†autonomy】.
L19: As Berlin showed, negative and positive liberty are not merely two distinct kinds of liberty; they can be seen as rival, incompatible interpretations of a single political ideal. Since few people claim to be against liberty, the way this term is interpreted and defined can have important political implications.
L20: Political 【10†liberalism】 tends to presuppose a negative definition of liberty: liberals generally claim that if one favors individual liberty one should place strong limitations on the activities of the state.
L21: Critics of liberalism often contest this implication by contesting the negative definition of liberty: they argue that the pursuit of liberty understood as self-realization or as self-determination (whether of the individual or of the collectivity) can require state intervention of a kind not normally allowed by liberals.
L22: Many authors prefer to talk of positive and negative freedom. This is only a difference of style, and the terms ‘liberty’ and ‘freedom’ are normally used interchangeably by political and social philosophers. Although some attempts have been made to distinguish between liberty and freedom (Pitkin 1988; Williams 2001; Dworkin 2011), generally speaking these have not caught on. Nor can they be translated into other European languages, which contain only the one term, of either Latin or Germanic origin (e.g.
L23: liberté, Freiheit), where English contains both.

L39: ## 1. Two Concepts of Liberty
L40: Imagine you are driving a car through town, and you come to a fork in the road. You turn left, but no one was forcing you to go one way or the other. Next you come to a crossroads. You turn right, but no one was preventing you from going left or straight on. There is no traffic to speak of and there are no diversions or police roadblocks. So you seem, as a driver, to be completely free.
L41: But this picture of your situation might change quite dramatically if we consider that the reason you went left and then right is that you’re addicted to cigarettes and you’re desperate to get to the tobacco shop before it closes. Rather than driving, you feel you are being driven, as your urge to smoke leads you uncontrollably to turn the wheel first to the left and then to the right.
L42: Moreover, you’re perfectly aware that your turning right at the crossroads means you’ll probably miss a train that was to take you to an appointment you care about very much. You long to be free of this irrational desire that is not only threatening your longevity but is also stopping you right now from doing what you think you ought to be doing.
L43: This story gives us two contrasting ways of thinking of liberty. On the one hand, one can think of liberty as the absence of obstacles external to the agent. You are free if no one is stopping you from doing whatever you might want to do. In the above story you appear, in this sense, to be free. On the other hand, one can think of liberty as the presence of control on the part of the agent.
L44: To be free, you must be self-determined, which is to say that you must be able to control your own destiny in your own interests. In the above story you appear, in this sense, to be unfree: you are not in control of your own destiny, as you are failing to control a passion that you yourself would rather be rid of and which is preventing you from realizing what you recognize to be your true interests.
L45: One might say that while on the first view liberty is simply about how many doors are open to the agent, on the second view it is more about going through the right doors for the right reasons.
L46: In a famous essay first published in 1958, Isaiah Berlin called these two concepts of liberty negative and positive respectively (Berlin 1969).^{[【23†1】]} The reason for using these labels is that in the first case liberty seems to be a mere absence of something (i.e. of obstacles, barriers, constraints or interference from others), whereas in the second case it seems to require the presence of something (i.e. of control, self-mastery, self-determination or self-realization).
L47: In Berlin’s words, we use the negative concept of liberty in attempting to answer the question ‘What is the area within which the subject—a person or group of persons—is or should be left to do or be what he is able to do or be, without interference by other persons?’, whereas we use the positive concept in attempting to answer the question ‘What, or who, is the source of control or interference that can determine someone to do, or be, this rather than that?’ (1969, pp. 121–22).
L48: It is useful to think of the difference between the two concepts in terms of the difference between factors that are external and factors that are internal to the agent. While theorists of negative freedom are primarily interested in the degree to which individuals or groups suffer interference from external bodies, theorists of positive freedom are more attentive to the internal factors affecting the degree to which individuals or groups act autonomously.
L49: Given this difference, one might be tempted to think that a political philosopher should concentrate exclusively on negative freedom, a concern with positive freedom being more relevant to psychology or individual morality than to political and social institutions.
L50: This, however, would be premature, for among the most hotly debated issues in political philosophy are the following: Is the positive concept of freedom a political concept? Can individuals or groups achieve positive freedom through political action? Is it possible for the state to promote the positive freedom of citizens on their behalf? And if so, is it desirable for the state to do so? The classic texts in the history of western political thought are divided over how these questions should be answered: theorists in the classical liberal tradition, like Benjamin Constant, Wilhelm von Humboldt, Herbert Spencer, and J.S.
L51: Mill, are typically classed as answering ‘no’ and therefore as defending a negative concept of political freedom; theorists that are critical of this tradition, like Jean-Jacques Rousseau, G.W.F. Hegel, Karl Marx and T.H. Green, are typically classed as answering ‘yes’ and as defending a positive concept of political freedom.
L52: In its political form, positive freedom has often been thought of as necessarily achieved through a collectivity. Perhaps the clearest case is that of Rousseau’s theory of freedom, according to which individual freedom is achieved through participation in the process whereby one’s community exercises collective control over its own affairs in accordance with the ‘general will’.
L53: Put in the simplest terms, one might say that a democratic society is a free society because it is a self-determined society, and that a member of that society is free to the extent that he or she participates in its democratic process. But there are also individualist applications of the concept of positive freedom. For example, it is sometimes said that a government should aim actively to create the conditions necessary for individuals to be self-sufficient or to achieve self-realization.
L54: The welfare state has sometimes been defended on this basis, as has the idea of a universal basic income. The negative concept of freedom, on the other hand, is most commonly assumed in liberal defenses of the constitutional liberties typical of liberal-democratic societies, such as freedom of movement, freedom of religion, and freedom of speech, and in arguments against paternalist or moralist state intervention. It is also often invoked in defenses of the right to private property.
L55: This said, some philosophers have contested the claim that private property necessarily enhances negative liberty (Cohen 1995, 2006), and still others have tried to show that negative liberty can ground a form of egalitarianism (Steiner 1994).
L56: After Berlin, the most widely cited and best developed analyses of the negative concept of liberty include Hayek (1960), Day (1970), Oppenheim (1981), Miller (1983), Steiner (1994), Carter (1999), and Kramer (2003). Among the most prominent contemporary analyses of the positive concept of liberty are Milne (1968), Gibbs (1976), C. Taylor (1979), Christman (1991, 2025), and Hirschmann (2003).
L57: ## 2. The Paradox of Positive Liberty
L58: Many liberals, including Berlin, have suggested that the positive concept of liberty carries with it a danger of authoritarianism. Consider the fate of a permanent and oppressed minority. Because the members of this minority participate in a democratic process characterized by majority rule, they might be said to be free on the grounds that they are members of a society exercising self-control over its own affairs. But they are oppressed, and so are surely unfree.
L59: Moreover, it is not necessary to see a society as democratic in order to see it as self-controlled; one might instead adopt an organic conception of society, according to which the collectivity is to be thought of as a living organism, and one might believe that this organism can be in control of itself only when its various parts are brought into line with some rational plan devised by its wise governors (who, to extend the metaphor, might be thought of as the organism’s brain).
L60: In this case, even the majority might be oppressed in the name of liberty.
L61: Such justifications of oppression in the name of liberty are no mere products of the liberal imagination, for there are notorious historical examples of their endorsement by authoritarian political leaders.
L62: Berlin, himself a liberal and writing during the cold war, was clearly moved by the way in which the apparently noble ideal of freedom as self-mastery or self-realization had been twisted and distorted by the totalitarian dictators of the twentieth century—most notably those of the Soviet Union—so as to claim that they, rather than the liberal West, were the true champions of freedom. The slippery slope towards this paradoxical conclusion begins, according to Berlin, with the idea of a divided self.
L63: To illustrate: the smoker in our story provides a clear example of a divided self, for she is both a self that desires to get to an appointment and a self that desires to get to the tobacconists, and these two desires are in conflict. We can now enrich this story in a plausible way by adding that one of these selves—the keeper of appointments—is superior to the other: the self that is a keeper of appointments is thus a ‘higher’ self, and the self that is a smoker is a ‘lower’ self.
L64: The higher self is the rational, reflecting self, the self that is capable of moral action and of taking responsibility for what she does. This is the true self, for rational reflection and moral responsibility are the features of humans that mark them off from other animals. The lower self, on the other hand, is the self of the passions, of unreflecting desires and irrational impulses.
L65: One is free, then, when one’s higher, rational self is in control and one is not a slave to one’s passions or to one’s merely empirical self. The next step down the slippery slope consists in pointing out that some individuals are more rational than others, and can therefore know best what is in their and others’ rational interests.
L66: This allows them to say that by forcing people less rational than themselves to do the rational thing and thus to realize their true selves, they are in fact liberating them from their merely empirical desires.
L67: Occasionally, Berlin says, the defender of positive freedom will take an additional step that consists in conceiving of the self as wider than the individual and as represented by an organic social whole—‘a tribe, a race, a church, a state, the great society of the living and the dead and the yet unborn’.
L68: The true interests of the individual are to be identified with the interests of this whole, and individuals can and should be coerced into fulfilling these interests, for they would not resist such overall plans for society if they were as rational and wise as the authors of those plans.
L69: ‘Once I take this view’, Berlin says, ‘I am in a position to ignore the actual wishes of men or societies, to bully, oppress, torture in the name, and on behalf, of their ‘real’ selves, in the secure knowledge that whatever is the true goal of man ... must be identical with his freedom’ (Berlin 1969, pp. 132–33).
L70: Those in the negative camp try to cut off this line of reasoning at the first step, by denying that there is any necessary relation between one’s freedom and one’s desires. Since one is free to the extent that one is externally unprevented from doing things, they say, one can be free to do what one does not desire to do. If being free meant being unprevented from realizing one’s desires, then one could, again paradoxically, reduce one’s unfreedom by coming to desire fewer of the things one is unfree to do.
L71: One could become free simply by contenting oneself with one’s situation. A perfectly contented slave is perfectly free to realize all of her desires. Nevertheless, we tend to think of slavery as the opposite of freedom. More generally, freedom is not to be confused with happiness, for in logical terms there is nothing to stop a free person from being unhappy or an unfree person from being happy. The happy person might feel free, but whether they are free is another matter (Day 1970).
L72: Proponents of the negative concept therefore tend to avoid saying that having freedom means being unprevented from doing as one desires. Instead, they say, it means being unprevented from doing whatever one might desire to do (Steiner 1994. Cf. Van Parijs 1995; Sugden 2006).
L73: Theorists of positive freedom might choose to bite the bullet and say that one can indeed become more free by desiring less (see e.g. Macmurray 1991, p.6), thus implying that the contented slave is free after all.^{[【24†2】]} To achieve freedom, they might say, the individual must learn, not so much to dominate certain merely empirical desires, as to rid herself of them. As Berlin puts it, if I have a wounded leg ‘there are two methods of freeing myself from pain. One is to heal the wound.
L74: But if the cure is too difficult or uncertain, there is another method. I can get rid of the wound by cutting off my leg’ (1969, pp. 135–36). Taken to its extreme, this strategy of liberation involves extinguishing as many of one’s own desires as possible. It is the strategy adopted by ascetics, stoics and Buddhist sages, and involves a ‘retreat into an inner citadel’—a soul or a purely noumenal self—in which the individual is immune to any outside forces.
L75: But this state, even if it can be achieved, is not one that liberals would want to call one of freedom, for it again risks masking important forms of oppression. After all, it is often in coming to terms with excessive external limitations in society that individuals retreat into themselves, pretending to themselves that they do not really desire the worldly goods or pleasures they have been denied.
L76: Moreover, the removal of desires may also be an effect of outside forces, such as brainwashing, which we should hardly want to call a realization of freedom.
L77: Because the concept of negative freedom concentrates on the external sphere in which individuals interact, it seems to provide a better guarantee against the dangers of paternalism and authoritarianism perceived by Berlin. To promote negative freedom is to promote the existence of a sphere of action within which the individual is sovereign, and within which she can pursue her own projects subject only to the constraint that she respect the spheres of others.
L78: Humboldt and Mill, both advocates of negative freedom, compared the development of an individual to that of a plant: individuals, like plants, must be allowed to grow, in the sense of developing their own faculties to the full and according to their own inner logic. Personal growth is something that cannot be imposed from without, but must come from within the individual.

If being free meant being unprevented from realizing one’s desires, then one could, again paradoxically, reduce one’s unfreedom by coming to desire fewer of the things one is unfree to do.
L71: One could become free simply by contenting oneself with one’s situation. A perfectly contented slave is perfectly free to realize all of her desires. Nevertheless, we tend to think of slavery as the opposite of freedom. More generally, freedom is not to be confused with happiness, for in logical terms there is nothing to stop a free person from being unhappy or an unfree person from being happy. The happy person might feel free, but whether they are free is another matter (Day 1970).
L72: Proponents of the negative concept therefore tend to avoid saying that having freedom means being unprevented from doing as one desires. Instead, they say, it means being unprevented from doing whatever one might desire to do (Steiner 1994. Cf. Van Parijs 1995; Sugden 2006).
L73: Theorists of positive freedom might choose to bite the bullet and say that one can indeed become more free by desiring less (see e.g. Macmurray 1991, p.6), thus implying that the contented slave is free after all.^{[【24†2】]} To achieve freedom, they might say, the individual must learn, not so much to dominate certain merely empirical desires, as to rid herself of them. As Berlin puts it, if I have a wounded leg ‘there are two methods of freeing myself from pain. One is to heal the wound.
L74: But if the cure is too difficult or uncertain, there is another method. I can get rid of the wound by cutting off my leg’ (1969, pp. 135–36). Taken to its extreme, this strategy of liberation involves extinguishing as many of one’s own desires as possible. It is the strategy adopted by ascetics, stoics and Buddhist sages, and involves a ‘retreat into an inner citadel’—a soul or a purely noumenal self—in which the individual is immune to any outside forces.
L75: But this state, even if it can be achieved, is not one that liberals would want to call one of freedom, for it again risks masking important forms of oppression. After all, it is often in coming to terms with excessive external limitations in society that individuals retreat into themselves, pretending to themselves that they do not really desire the worldly goods or pleasures they have been denied.
L76: Moreover, the removal of desires may also be an effect of outside forces, such as brainwashing, which we should hardly want to call a realization of freedom.
L77: Because the concept of negative freedom concentrates on the external sphere in which individuals interact, it seems to provide a better guarantee against the dangers of paternalism and authoritarianism perceived by Berlin. To promote negative freedom is to promote the existence of a sphere of action within which the individual is sovereign, and within which she can pursue her own projects subject only to the constraint that she respect the spheres of others.
L78: Humboldt and Mill, both advocates of negative freedom, compared the development of an individual to that of a plant: individuals, like plants, must be allowed to grow, in the sense of developing their own faculties to the full and according to their own inner logic. Personal growth is something that cannot be imposed from without, but must come from within the individual.
L79: ## 3. Rescuing Positive Liberty
L80: Much of the more recent work on positive liberty has been motivated by a dissatisfaction with the ideal of negative liberty combined with an awareness of the abuses to which the positive concept is vulnerable, as argued by Berlin. Consider the ideal defended by Humboldt and Mill, mentioned at the end of the previous section.
L81: This ideal, it might be argued, looks more like a positive concept of liberty than a negative one, for positive liberty consists in exactly this growth of the individual: the free individual is one that develops, determines and changes her own desires and interests autonomously and from within. This is not liberty as the mere absence of obstacles, but liberty as autonomy or self-realization.
L82: Moreover, the promotion of this kind of individual development does not necessarily license the authoritarian or paternalistic outcomes feared by Berlin.
L83: Along these lines, John Christman (1991, 2005, 2009, 2013, 2025) has argued that positive liberty concerns the ways in which desires are formed—whether as a result of rational reflection on all the options available, or as a result of pressure, manipulation or ignorance. What it does not regard, he says, is the content of an individual’s desires.
L84: The promotion of positive freedom need not therefore involve the claim that there is only one right answer to the question of how a person should live, nor need it allow, or even be compatible with, a society’s forcing its members into given patterns of behavior. Take the example of a Muslim woman who claims to espouse the fundamentalist doctrines generally followed by her family and the community in which she lives.
L85: On Christman’s account, this person is positively unfree if her desire to conform was somehow oppressively imposed upon her through indoctrination, manipulation or deceit. She is positively free, on the other hand, if she arrived at her desire to conform while aware of other reasonable options, if those options were genuinely available to her, and if she weighed and assessed them rationally.
L86: Even if this woman seems to prefer subservient behavior, there is nothing necessarily freedom-enhancing or freedom-restricting about her having the desires she has, since freedom regards not the content of these desires but their mode of formation. On this view, forcing her to do certain things rather than others can never make her more free, and Berlin’s paradox of positive freedom would seem to have been avoided.
L87: This more procedural account of positive liberty allows us to point to kinds of internal constraint that seem to fall off the radar if we adopt only the negative concept. For example, some radical political theorists believe it can help us to make sense of forms of oppression and structural injustice that cannot be traced to overt acts of prevention or coercion.
L88: On the one hand, in agreement with Berlin, we should recognize the dangers that come with promoting the values or interests of a person’s ‘true self’ in opposition to what they manifestly desire. Thus, the procedural account avoids all reference to a true self. On the other, we should recognize that people’s actual selves are inevitably formed in a social context and that their values and senses of identity (for example, in terms of gender or race or nationality) are shaped by cultural influences.
L89: In this sense, the self is socially constructed, and the social construction can itself occur in oppressive ways.
L90: The challenge, then, is to show how a person’s values can be thus shaped but without the kind of oppressive imposition or manipulation that comes not only from political coercion but also, more subtly, from practices or institutions that stigmatize or marginalize certain identities or that attach costs to the endorsement of values deviating from acceptable norms, for these kinds of imposition or manipulation can be just another way of promoting a substantive ideal of the self.
L91: And this was exactly the danger against which Berlin was warning, except that the danger is less visible and can be created unintentionally (Christman 2013, 2022; Hirschmann 2003, 2013; Coole 2013).
L92: While this theory of positive freedom undoubtedly provides a tool for criticizing the limiting effects of certain practices and institutions in contemporary liberal societies, it remains to be seen what kinds of political action can be pursued in order to promote content-neutral positive liberty without encroaching on any individual’s rightful sphere of negative liberty.
L93: Thus, the potential conflict between the two ideals of negative and positive freedom might survive Christman’s alternative analysis, albeit in a milder form.
L94: Even if we rule out coercing individuals into specific patterns of behavior, a state interested in promoting content-neutral positive liberty might still have considerable space for intervention aimed at public enlightenment, perhaps subsidizing some kinds of activities (in order to encourage a plurality of genuine options) and financing such intervention through taxation.
L95: Liberals might criticize this kind of intervention on anti-paternalist grounds, objecting that such measures will require the state to use resources in ways that the supposedly heteronomous individuals, if left to themselves, might have chosen to spend in other ways.
L96: In other words, even in its content-neutral form, the ideal of positive freedom might still conflict with the liberal idea of respect for persons, one interpretation of which involves viewing individuals from the outside and taking their choices at face value. From a liberal point of view, the blindness to internal constraints can be intentional (Carter 2011a).
L97: Some liberals will make an exception to this restriction on state intervention in the case of the education of children, in such a way as to provide for the active cultivation of open minds and rational reflection (see e.g., Clayton 2006). Even here, however, other liberals will object that the right to negative liberty includes the right to decide how one’s children should be educated (see e.g. Galston 2002, and for counterarguments, Clayton 2006, ch 3).
L98: Is it necessary to refer to internal constraints in order to make sense of the phenomena of oppression and structural injustice? Some might contest this view, or say that it is true only up to a point, for there are at least two reasons for thinking that the oppressed are lacking in negative liberty.
L99: First, while Berlin himself equated economic and social disadvantages with natural disabilities, claiming that neither represented constraints on negative liberty but only on personal abilities, many theorists of negative liberty disagree: if I lack the money to buy a jacket from a clothes shop, then any attempt on my part to carry away the jacket is likely to meet with preventive actions or punishment on the part of the shop keeper or the agents of the state.
L100: This is a case of interpersonal interference, not merely of personal inability. In the normal circumstances of a market economy, purchasing power is indeed a very reliable indicator of how far other people will stop you from doing certain things if you try. It is therefore strongly correlated with degrees of negative freedom (Cohen 1995, 2011; Waldron 1993; Carter 2007; Grant 2013).
L101: Thus, while the promotion of content-neutral positive liberty might imply the transfer of certain kinds of resources to members of disadvantaged groups, the same might be true of the promotion of negative liberty. Second, negative unfreedom can be a condition of disadvantaged groups as well as of their individual members. Some social structures may be such as to tolerate the liberation of only a limited number of members of a given group. G.A.
L102: Cohen famously focused on the case of proletarians who can escape their condition by successfully setting up a business of their own through a mixture of hard work and luck.
L103: In such cases, while each individual member of the disadvantaged group might be negatively free in the sense of being unprevented from choosing this path of liberation from their proletarian condition, the freedom of each individual is conditional on the unfreedom of the majority of the rest of the group, since not all can escape in this way.
L104: Each individual member of the class therefore partakes in a form of collective negative unfreedom (Cohen 1988, 2006; for discussion see Mason 1996; Hindriks 2008; Grant 2013; Schmidt 2023).
L105: ## 4. Republican Liberty: A Third Way?
L106: Some philosophers have rejected both the negative and the positive ideals of freedom, claiming that liberty is not merely the enjoyment of a sphere of non-interference but the enjoyment of certain conditions in which such non-interference is guaranteed. These conditions may include a democratic constitution and a series of safeguards against a government wielding power arbitrarily, such as popular control and the separation of powers.
L107: As Berlin points out, there is no conceptually necessary connection between negative liberty and any particular form of government (see also Hayek 1960). Yet it may seem counterintuitive to say that I can in theory be free to a significant degree while living under a dictatorship, or that a slave can enjoy considerable liberty as long as the slave-owner is benevolent.
L108: Would my subjection to the arbitrary power of a dictator or slave-owner not be sufficient in itself to qualify me as unfree? If it would be, then the enjoyment of freedom must require the presence of institutions that secure each citizen’s independence from such arbitrary power.
L109: This kind of freedom is commonly referred to as ‘republican’, and its foremost proponent is Philip Pettit (1997, 2001, 2014; see also Skinner 1998, 2002; Weinstock and Nadeau 2004; Laborde and Maynor 2008; Lovett 2010, 2022; Breen and McBride 2015; List and Valentini 2016).
L110: Republican freedom can be understood as a kind of status: to be a free person is to enjoy the rights and privileges attached to the status of republican citizenship, whereas the paradigm of unfreedom is having the status of slave. Even if the slave enjoys non-interference, she is, as Pettit puts it, ‘dominated’, because she is subject to the arbitrary power of her owner, even if that power is never in fact exercised. Republicans therefore define freedom as ‘non-domination’.
L111: Non-domination is distinct from negative freedom, Pettit says, for two reasons. First, one can enjoy non-interference without enjoying non-domination, as shown by the above example of the slave with a non-interfering master. Second, one can enjoy non-domination while nevertheless being interfered with, provided that such interference is constrained to track one’s avowed interests thanks to republican power structures: only arbitrary power is inimical to freedom, not power as such.
L112: Republican freedom is also distinct from positive freedom as expounded and criticized by Berlin. First, republican freedom does not consist in the activity of virtuous political participation, although such participation is seen as instrumentally related to freedom as non-domination.
L113: Second, republican freedom cannot lead to anything like the oppressive consequences Berlin associated with positive liberty, because it has a commitment to non-domination and to liberal-democratic institutions already built into it. This last point also makes it quite different from Christman’s concept of positive freedom. Christman argues for the compatibility of positive freedom with liberalism, but not by pointing to a conceptual link between freedom and liberal-democratic institutions.
L114: Much of the appeal of freedom as non-domination lies in its apparent ability to capture phenomena such as oppression and structural injustice without appealing to internal constraints. It has been applied not only to relations of domination between governments and citizens, but also to relations of domination between employers and workers (Breen and McBride 2015), between husbands and wives (Lovett 2022), and between able-bodied and disabled people (De Wispelaere and Casassas 2014; Sépulchre 2022).
L115: It remains to be seen, however, whether republican freedom is ultimately distinguishable from negative freedom, or whether republicans have instead simply provided good arguments to the effect that negative freedom is best promoted, on balance and over time, through certain kinds of political institutions rather than others. While there is no conceptually necessary connection between negative liberty and democratic government, there may nevertheless be a strong empirical correlation between the two.
L116: Many theorists have argued that republican policies are therefore best defended empirically on the basis of negative freedom, rather than by rejecting that concept (Carter 1999, 2008; Kramer 2003, 2008, Goodin and Jackson 2007; Moen 2024). An important premise in such an argument is that the extent of a person’s negative freedom is a function not simply of how many single actions are prevented, but of how many different act-combinations are prevented.
L117: People who can achieve their goals only by bowing and scraping to their masters must be seen as less free, negatively, than people who can achieve those goals unconditionally. Another important premise is that the extent to which people are negatively free depends, in part, on the probability with which they would be constrained from performing future acts or act-combinations should they attempt to do so.
L118: People who are subject to arbitrary power are less free in the negative sense, even if they do not actually suffer interference, because the probability of their suffering constraints is always greater (ceteris paribus, as a matter of empirical fact) than it would be if they were not subject to that arbitrary power.
L119: Only this greater probability, these liberal theorists say, can adequately explain the concerns expressed by republicans about the fear, the sense of exposure, and the precariousness of the dominated (see Bruin 2009; Lang 2012; Shnayderman 2012; Kirby 2016; Carter and Shnayderman 2019).
L120: In reply, republicans have insisted that freedom as non-domination is distinct from negative liberty because what matters for an agent’s freedom is not the improbability but the impossibility of others interfering.
L121: For example, a husband might be kind and generous, and therefore be extremely unlikely ever to deny his wife the same opportunities as he himself enjoys; but the wife is still dominated if the structure of norms in her society is such as to permit husbands to frustrate the choices of their wives in numerous ways. If she lives in such a society, she is still subject to the husband’s power whether he likes it or not.
L122: Moreover, the wife’s subjection to his power will tend to influence how third parties treat her – for example, in terms of offering employment opportunities.
L123: Taken at face value, however, the requirement of impossibility of interference seems over-demanding, as it is never completely impossible for others to constrain me. For example, it is not impossible that I be stabbed by someone as I walk down the street this afternoon. If the mere possibility of the stabbing makes me unfree to walk down the street, then unfreedom is everywhere and the achievement of freedom is impossible.
L124: To avoid this worry, republicans have qualified their impossibility requirement: for me to be free to walk down the street, it must be impossible for others to stab me with impunity (Pettit 2008a, 2008b; Skinner 2008). But this qualification is open to objections.
L125: Is impunity a purely formal requirement, or should we say that no one can carry out a street stabbing with impunity if, say, at least 70% of such stabbings lead to prosecution? Even if 100% of such stabbings lead to prosecution, there will still be some stabbings. Will they not be sources of unfreedom for the victims?
L126: Another problem with the requirement of impossibility is that there will always be some group of individuals who, if they coordinated, could interfere arbitrarily with me. Such coalitions are possible, even if unlikely. Since no one is powerful enough to resist the interference of all such possible coalitions, not only the weak but also the powerful turn out to be dominated by others who might act in concert.
L127: Domination is thus pervasive, and republican freedom impossible to achieve (Dowding 2011; Simpson 2017). To avoid this conclusion, republicans have argued that not all potential coalitions possess dominating power. Consider two would-be muggers who might wish to coordinate an attack on me. They form a potential team but, it might be said, they do not dominate me unless they share an awareness of one another’s intentions and have a salient strategy for acting together.
L128: When such epistemic conditions are met, they constitute a capable team, and only capable teams dominate. Non-domination, while demanding, is therefore achievable (Lovett and Pettit 2018). Yet this reply has something of an ad hoc feel. Moreover, it does not conclusively resolve the problem, since sufficiently large or suitably situated groups will still qualify as capable teams. Too many agents seem to retain the capacity to form dominating coalitions (Simpson 2019).
L129: A more recent republican response to the impossibility problem invokes the concept of ‘ignorability’ (Ingham and Lovett 2019). The idea is that each potentially interfering agent has an actual type, consisting in her actual preferences regarding whether to interfere, and a range of possible types corresponding to preferences she might have in other possible worlds. There are some possible interfering types that can be ignored and therefore need not be considered sources of domination.
L130: They can be ignored when the common knowledge of a discrepancy between the actual type and the possible interfering types does not have significant practical consequences.
L131: To illustrate: in a traditional patriarchal society where husbands are generally expected or permitted to relate to their wives in coercive or exploitative ways, the news that a particular husband is benevolent will have significant practical consequences: third parties will treat his wife as more autonomous and, say, offer her employment. His possible interfering types are therefore not ignorable, and she is dominated.
L132: By contrast, in a liberal society like San Francisco, where husbands are generally expected to be non-interfering, the same news changes nothing significant in anyone’s behavior. His possible interfering types are therefore ignorable, and his wife is free. Hence, a possible interfering type is ignorable if and only if its becoming common knowledge that the agent’s actual type does not belong to that set of possible interfering types would have no significant practical consequences.
L133: By narrowing the range of possible worlds that freedom pertains to, the notion of ignorability is intended to make room for the possibility of republican freedom.
L134: Negative liberty theorists have raised two main objections to this move (Jafarov and Huseynli 2022). The first is that the criterion of ignorability is reducible to a threshold of sufficiently low probability. To say that the absence of certain interfering types would have ‘no significant practical consequences’ is, in effect, to say that interference is already unlikely enough to be disregarded by rational agents.
L135: Ignorability therefore collapses into the probabilistic standard that republicans sought to reject. The second objection challenges the claim that ignorability entails non-domination (assuming the republican account of domination). There are cases in which an agent’s possible interfering types are ignorable, yet domination persists because the agent retains an insufficiently constrained capacity to interfere.

L140: ## 5. One Concept of Liberty: Freedom as a Triadic Relation
L141: The two sides identified by Berlin disagree over which of two different concepts best captures the political ideal of liberty.
L142: Does this fact not denote the presence of some more basic agreement between the two sides? How, after all, could they see their disagreement as one about the nature of liberty if they did not think of themselves as in some sense talking about the same thing? In an influential article, the American legal philosopher Gerald MacCallum (1967) put forward the following answer: there is in fact only one basic concept of freedom, on which both sides in the debate converge.
L143: What the so-called negative and positive theorists disagree about is how this single concept of freedom should be interpreted. Indeed, in MacCallum’s view, there are a great many different possible interpretations of freedom, and it is only Berlin’s artificial dichotomy that has led us to think in terms of there being two.
L144: MacCallum defines the basic concept of freedom—the concept on which everyone agrees—as follows: a subject, or agent, is free from certain constraints, or preventing conditions, to do or become certain things. Freedom is therefore a triadic relation—that is, a relation between three things: an agent, certain preventing conditions, and certain doings or becomings of the agent.
L145: Any statement about freedom or unfreedom can be translated into a statement of the above form by specifying what is free or unfree, from what it is free or unfree, and what it is free or unfree to do or become. Any claim about the presence or absence of freedom in a given situation will therefore make certain assumptions about what counts as an agent, what counts as a constraint or limitation on freedom, and what counts as a purpose that the agent can be described as either free or unfree to carry out.
L146: The definition of freedom as a triadic relation was first put forward in the seminal work of Felix Oppenheim in the 1950s and ’60s. Oppenheim saw that an important meaning of ‘freedom’ in the context of political and social philosophy was as a relation between two agents and a particular (impeded or unimpeded) action. However, Oppenheim’s interpretation of freedom was an example of what Berlin would call a negative concept.
L147: What MacCallum did was to generalize this triadic structure so that it would cover all possible claims about freedom, whether of the negative or the positive variety. In MacCallum’s framework, unlike in Oppenheim’s, the interpretation of each of the three variables is left open. In other words, MacCallum’s position is a meta-theoretical one: his is a theory about the differences between theorists of freedom.
L148: To illustrate MacCallum’s point, let us return to the example of the smoker driving to the tobacco shop. In describing this person as either free or unfree, we make assumptions about each of MacCallum’s three variables. If we say that the driver is free, what we probably mean is that an agent, consisting in the driver’s empirical self, is free from external (physical or legal) obstacles to do whatever he or she might want to do.
L149: If, on the other hand, we say that the driver is unfree, what we probably mean is that an agent, consisting in a higher or rational self, is made unfree by internal, psychological constraints to carry out some rational, authentic or virtuous plan. Notice that in both claims there is a negative element and a positive element: each claim about freedom assumes both that freedom is freedom from something (i.e., preventing conditions) and that it is freedom to do or become something.
L150: The dichotomy between freedom from and freedom to is therefore a false one, and it is misleading to say that those who see the driver as free employ a negative concept and those who see the driver as unfree employ a positive one. What these two camps differ over is the way in which one should interpret each of the three variables in the triadic freedom-relation. More precisely, what they differ over is the extension to be assigned to each of the variables.
L151: Thus, those whom Berlin places in the negative camp typically conceive of the agent as having the same extension as that which it is generally given in ordinary discourse: they tend to think of the agent as an individual human being and as including all of the empirical beliefs and desires of that individual.
L152: Those in the so-called positive camp, on the other hand, often depart from the ordinary notion, in one sense imagining the agent as more extensive than in the ordinary notion, and in another sense imagining it as less extensive: they think of the agent as having a greater extension than in ordinary discourse in cases where they identify the agent’s true desires and aims with those of some collectivity of which she is a member; and they think of the agent as having a lesser extension than in ordinary discourse in cases where they identify the true agent with only a subset of her empirical beliefs and desires—i.e., with those that are rational, authentic or virtuous.
L153: Secondly, those in Berlin’s positive camp tend to take a wider view of what counts as a constraint on freedom than those in his negative camp: the set of relevant obstacles is more extensive for the former than for the latter, since negative theorists tend to count only external obstacles as constraints on freedom, whereas positive theorists also allow that one may be constrained by internal factors, such as irrational desires, fears or ignorance.
L154: And thirdly, those in Berlin’s positive camp tend to take a narrower view of what counts as a purpose one can be free to fulfill. The set of relevant purposes is less extensive for them than for the negative theorists, for we have seen that they tend to restrict the relevant set of actions or states to those that are rational, authentic or virtuous, whereas those in the negative camp tend to extend this variable so as to cover any action or state the agent might desire.
L155: On MacCallum’s analysis, then, there is no simple dichotomy between positive and negative liberty; rather, we should recognize that there is a whole range of possible interpretations or conceptions of the single concept of liberty. (The distinction between a concept and its various conceptions comes from Rawls 1971, p.5. A number of authors have applied it to the concept of liberty, explicating that concept by means of MacCallum’s triadic formula—see e.g. Gray 1991.)
L156: ## 6. The Analysis of Constraints
L157: To illustrate the range of conceptions of the concept of liberty made available by MacCallum’s analysis, let us now take a closer look at his second variable—that of constraints. These can be classified along two logically independent dimensions: their sources and their types.
L158: By constraint-source we mean the factors that bring about a constraint: must an obstacle be attributable to the conduct of other persons in order to count as a constraint on freedom, or can purely natural events and self-inflicted incapacities also render one unfree? By constraint-types we mean classes of constraints identified in terms of their intrinsic properties: are constraints on freedom limited to physical barriers such as walls or locked doors, or can they include mental barriers such as phobias?; and does a constraint on freedom necessarily render an action impossible, or does it suffice that it make the action more difficult or costly?
L159: ### 6.1 Sources of Constraint
L160: Most proponents of negative conceptions—including, as we have seen, Berlin—hold that freedom is essentially a social relation. They therefore limit the range of obstacles that count as constraints on freedom to those attributable to the conduct of other agents. If I am incapacitated by natural causes—by a genetic handicap, say, or by being struck by lightning—I may be rendered unable to do certain things, but I am not, for that reason, rendered unfree to do them.
L161: This observation yields a distinction between unfreedoms, understood as inabilities due to the conduct of other persons, and mere inabilities, which are due to nature or to oneself. If you lock me in my house, I am both unable and unfree to leave. But if I am unable to leave because of a naturally caused illness or a snow drift which has blocked the door from the outside, or because I locked the door from the inside and destroyed the key, I am not unfree to leave.
L162: There are different ways of cashing out the idea that the source of unfreedom is the conduct of other persons. One possibility is the causal view, according to which I am unfree to do something whenever my inability is causally attributable to another person’s action (Oppenheim 1961, 1985; Steiner 1994; Kramer 2003).
L163: Another possibility is the moral responsibility view, according to which we must ask whether the agent who created the obstacle can be held morally responsible for my inability (Kristjánsson 1996; Miller 1983; Shnayderman 2013; Schmidt 2016).
L164: The latter view makes freedom partly dependent on moral judgments, whereas the former treats freedom as an empirical behavioural concept, requiring only knowledge of causal relations, and therefore allows persons with different moral judgements to agree on whether someone is unfree.
L165: It has recently been suggested that if moral responsibility is understood broadly, as moral answerability, the two views may converge, since the set of actions for which agents are answerable is arguably coextensive with the set of actions that generate unfreedom under the causality view.
L166: On this capacious account, agents can, in principle, be asked to answer for any obstacle their actions causally bring about, where answerability does not entail any specific moral response such as blame or praise (Huseynli 2026).
L167: The relevant conduct of other agents might be said to include only actions, or both actions and omissions. Suppose I am unable to leave my house because a snow drift has blocked the door, but you, being outside, could easily remove the snow. If you fail to do so, does your omission render me unfree? Some theorists answer that it does not render me unfree (Oppenheim 1985; Steiner 1994; Kramer 2003; Shnayderman 2013).
L168: Others argue that certain omissions, especially those for which agents can be held responsible, or those involving a failure to intervene where obstacles are easily removable, do count as sources of unfreedom (Kristjánsson 1996; Miller 1985; Christman 2025).
L169: Among those who agree that only the conduct of others generates unfreedom, there is further disagreement about what we might call the conditions of freedom. If I suffer from a natural or self-inflicted inability to do something, I am not unfree to do it, if unfreedom is understood as a social relation.
L170: But should we say that I therefore remain free to do it? Or should we instead say that the inability entails my lack of freedom to do it without entailing my unfreedom to do it? The former position implies a ‘bivalent’ conception of freedom, according to which a person is necessarily either free or unfree to do something (Steiner 2001; Carter and Steiner 2022). The latter position implies a ‘trivalent’ conception, according to which there are some things that a person is neither free nor unfree to do.
L171: Kramer (2003) endorses a trivalent conception whereby freedom is identified with ability, and unfreedom with the prevention by others of outcomes that the agent would otherwise be able to bring about. The unfreedom to do x is a social relation; the freedom to do x is not.
L172: Some theorists go even further, arguing that we should not only identify freedom with ability, but we should also identify unfreedom with inability, thereby denying any distinction between unfreedoms and mere inabilities (Crocker 1980; Cohen 2011, pp. 193–97; Sen 1992; Van Parijs 1995; Garnett 2022a; Rainbolt 2024).
L173: The main motivation for this bivalent ability-based conception of freedom is that the distinction between unfreedom and mere inability lacks a principled justification and may reflect ideological bias, implying that mere inabilities are somehow less important than social unfreedoms. Bivalent freedom-as-ability is sometimes referred to as a kind of positive freedom—somewhat confusingly, given that it is very different from what Berlin meant by positive freedom.
L174: It is generally assumed in defenses of the so-called capability approach to justice and human development (Sen 1985, 1988, 1992; Nussbaum, 2006, 2011).
L175: The question of sources has important implications for how we assess economic constraints like recession, poverty and unemployment. Do such conditions merely render people unable to act, or do they also render them unfree? Libertarians and egalitarians have often provided contrasting answers to this question by appealing to different conceptions of the sources of constraints.
L176: One way of denying that such economic constraints produce unfreedom is to adopt an even more restrictive view of its sources, counting only those obstacles that are intentionally brought about by other persons. In this case, impersonal economic forces, being brought about unintentionally, can restrict people’s options without making them any less free.
L177: This view has been adopted by several market-oriented libertarians, including, most famously, Friedrich von Hayek (1960, 1982), according to whom freedom is the absence of coercion, understood as subjection to the arbitrary will of another. (Notice the somewhat surprising similarity between this conception of freedom and the republican conception discussed earlier, in section 4).
L178: This narrow conception of a constraint is contested not only by those adopting the notion of freedom as ability, but also by those adopting a broader conception of the sources of social unfreedom. Notice, moreover, that economic hardship can be understood as creating unfreedom on both the causal view and the moral responsibility view of social freedom (see Cohen 2011 cited at the end of section 3).
L179: But why would one want to distinguish, in the first place, between unfreedom and mere inability? The mere claim that freedom is a social relation looks more like a stipulation than a justification. An answer might be found by examining more closely our ideas about the value of freedom.
L180: Freedom might be said to have ‘ability-independent’ value: even when we are unable to act, the fact that our inability is not due to the conduct of others can itself be something that matters morally (Morriss 2012; Intropi 2022; Huseynli 2025). One reason for thinking this might be that abstaining from constraining others is part of what respectful conduct amounts to.
L181: It would explain why individuals may object to limits imposed by the law or the conduct of other individuals even where they already lacked the relevant abilities. The idea of freedom’s ability-independent value supports the idea that debates about freedom cannot be reduced to those about ability or capability, since the normative significance of social freedom extends beyond what persons can do and become, to how they stand in relation to others.
L182: ### 6.2 Distinguishing Constraint Sources and Constraint Types
L183: Constraint types and constraint sources are often not distinguished carefully. For example, those who interpret freedom as a social relation are often said to count only those obstacles that are external to the agent. But the term ‘external’ is ambiguous in this context, for it might refer either to the location of the obstacle itself, or to the location of its source (this ambiguity was present in the brief summary in section 1).
L184: Obstacles that count as internal in terms of their location include psychological phenomena such as ignorance, irrational desires, illusions and phobias. Such obstacles can arise in various ways: for example, they might have a genetic origin, or they might be brought about intentionally by others, as in the case of brainwashing or manipulation.
L185: In the first case we have an internal constraint brought about by features of the agent herself, and in this sense internally; in the second, an internal constraint intentionally imposed by another agent, and in this sense externally. The two dimensions of type and source are therefore logically independent: it is theoretically possible to combine a narrow view of what counts as a source of a constraint with a broad view of what types of obstacle count as unfreedom-generating constraints, or vice versa.
L186: As a result, it is not clear that theorists who are normally placed in the negative camp need deny the existence of constraints on freedom that are located internally (see Kramer 2003; Garnett 2007).
L187: To illustrate the independence of the two dimensions of type and source, let us compare the case of Hayek, cited in the previous subsection, with that of the unorthodox libertarian Hillel Steiner (1974–5, 1994).
L188: On the one hand, Steiner has a much broader view than Hayek of the possible sources of constraints on freedom: he does not limit the set of such sources to intentional actions of other persons, but extends it to cover all actions of other agents, believing that any limitation of such social sources can only be an arbitrary stipulation, usually arising from some ideological bias.
L189: On the other hand, Steiner has an even narrower view than Hayek about what type of obstacle counts as a constraint on freedom: for Steiner, an agent counts as unfree to do something only if it is physically impossible for her to do that thing. Any extension of the constraint variable to include other types of obstacle, such as the costs anticipated in coercive threats, would, in his view, necessarily involve a reference to the agent’s desires, and we have seen (in sec.
L190: 2) that for those liberals in the negative camp there is no conceptually necessary relation between an agent’s freedom and her desires. Consider the coercive threat ‘Your money or your life!’. This does not make it impossible for you to refuse to hand over your money, only much less desirable for you to do so. If you decide not to hand over the money, you will suffer the cost of being killed.
L191: That will count as a restriction of your freedom, because it will render physically impossible a great number of actions on your part. But it is not the issuing of the threat that creates this unfreedom, and you are not unfree until the sanction (described in the threat) is carried out. For this reason, Steiner excludes threats—and with them all other kinds of imposed costs—from the set of obstacles that count as freedom-restricting. This conception of freedom derives from Hobbes (Leviathan, chs.
L192: 14 and 21), and its defenders often call it the pure negative conception (M. Taylor 1982; Steiner 1994; Carter and Kramer 2008; cf. Flathman 1987) to distinguish it from those impure negative conceptions that make at least minimal references to the agent’s beliefs, desires or values (Oppenheim 1961, 1981; Benn and Weinstein 1971; Miller 1983).
L193: Steiner’s account of the relation between freedom and coercive threats might be thought to have counterintuitive implications. Many laws that are normally thought to restrict negative freedom do not physically prevent people from doing what is prohibited, but deter them from doing so by threatening punishment.
L194: Are we to say, then, that these laws do not restrict the negative freedom of those who obey them? A solution to this problem may consist in saying that although a law against doing some action, x, does not remove the freedom to do x, it nevertheless renders physically impossible certain combinations of actions that include doing x and doing what would be precluded by the punishment. There is a restriction of the person’s overall negative freedom—i.e.
L195: a reduction in the overall number of act-combinations available to her—even though she does not lose the freedom to do any specific thing considered in isolation (Carter 1999).
L196: ## 7. The Concept of Overall Freedom
L197: The concept of overall freedom appears to play an important role both in everyday discourse and in contemporary political philosophy. We commonly talk about people’s freedom as increasing or decreasing, we say that one society is freer than another, and so on.
L198: Yet it is only over the last three decades that philosophers have moved beyond the question of what it is to be free to do or become this or that particular thing, in order to ask, in addition, whether we can make sense of descriptive claims to the effect that one person or society is freer than another, or of liberal normative claims to the effect that freedom should be maximized or that people should enjoy equal freedom or that they each have a right to a certain minimum level of freedom.
L199: The literal meaningfulness of such claims depends on the possibility of gauging degrees of overall freedom, sometimes comparatively, sometimes absolutely.
L200: Theorists disagree, however, about the importance of the notion of overall freedom. For some libertarian and liberal egalitarian theorists, freedom is valuable as such. This suggests that more freedom is better than less (at least ceteris paribus), and that freedom is one of those goods that a liberal society ought to distribute in a certain way among individuals.
L201: For other liberal theorists, like Ronald Dworkin (1977, 2011) and the later Rawls (1991), freedom is not valuable as such, and all claims about maximal or equal freedom ought to be interpreted not as literal references to a scalar good called ‘liberty’ but as elliptical references to the adequacy of this or that list of specific liberties, or types of liberties, selected on the basis of values other than liberty itself. Only the first group of theorists finds the notion of overall freedom interesting.
L202: The theoretical problems involved in measuring overall freedom include that of how an agent’s available actions are to be individuated, counted and weighted, and that of comparing and weighting different constraint types (but not necessarily different constraint sources) such as physical prevention, punishability, threats and manipulation).
L203: How are we to make sense of the claim that the number of options available to a person has increased? Should all options count for the same in terms of degrees of freedom, or should they be weighted according to their importance in terms of other values? If the latter, does the notion of overall freedom really add anything of substance to the idea that people should be granted those specific freedoms that are valuable? Should the degree of variety among options also count? And how are we to compare the unfreedom created by the physical impossibility of an action with, say, the unfreedom created by the difficulty or costliness or punishability of an action? It is only by comparing these different kinds of actions and constraints that we shall be in a position to compare individuals’ overall degrees of freedom.
L204: These problems have been addressed, with differing degrees of optimism, not only by mainstream political philosophers (Steiner 1983; Carter 1999; Kramer 2003; Garnett 2016; Côté 2020; Carter and Steiner 2022) but also by theorists working at the interface between philosophy and economics, including social choice theorists interested in finding a freedom-based alternative to the standard utilitarian or welfarist framework that has tended to dominate their discipline (e.g.
L205: Pattanaik and Xu 1990, 1998; Hees 2000; Sen 2002; Sugden 1998, 2003, 2006; Bavetta 2004; Bavetta and Navarra 2012, 2014). A particularly important line of research within this social choice approach concerns the relevance of diversity of available options for the measurement of freedom (See e.g. Hees 2004; Sher 2021; Gravel et al. 2025; Côté 2025).
L206: MacCallum’s framework is particularly well suited to the clarification of such issues. For this reason, theorists working on the measurement of freedom tend not to refer a great deal to the distinction between positive and negative freedom. Most of them are concerned with the notion of freedom understood as the availability of options.
L207: This notion of freedom is unequivocally negative in Berlin’s sense at least where two conditions are met: first, the source of unfreedom is limited to the actions of other agents, so that natural or self-inflicted obstacles are not seen as decreasing an agent’s overall freedom; second, the actions one is free or unfree to perform are weighted in some value-neutral way, so that one is not seen as freer simply because the options available to one are more valuable or conducive to one’s self-realization.
L208: Of the above-mentioned authors, only Steiner embraces both conditions explicitly. Sen rejects both of them, despite not endorsing anything like positive freedom in Berlin’s sense. Many social choice theorists seem to treat freedom as a dyadic relation, either assuming that freedom is identical to ability or remaining agnostic about the interpretation of MacCallum’s constraint variable.
L209: ## 8. Is the Distinction Still Useful?
L210: We began with a simple distinction between two concepts of liberty, and have progressed from this to the recognition that liberty might be conceptualized in any number of ways, depending on how one interprets the three variables of agent, constraints, and purposes. Negative liberty is the absence of constraints, but so is positive liberty. Positive liberty is the possibility of realizing certain ends, but so is negative liberty.

L210: We began with a simple distinction between two concepts of liberty, and have progressed from this to the recognition that liberty might be conceptualized in any number of ways, depending on how one interprets the three variables of agent, constraints, and purposes. Negative liberty is the absence of constraints, but so is positive liberty. Positive liberty is the possibility of realizing certain ends, but so is negative liberty.
L211: Despite the utility of MacCallum’s triadic formula and its strong influence on analytic philosophers, however, Berlin’s distinction has remained an important point of reference in contemporary debates. Is this continued use of the distinction philosophically well-founded? Let us consider some possible reasons for this persistence, while also noting their conceptual limits in light of the above discussion.
L212: A first reason for the persistence of the distinction might be thought to lie in the difference between what Charles Taylor, in his defense of positive liberty, called an opportunity concept and an exercise concept of freedom (C. Taylor 1979).
L213: If one thinks of freedom as an opportunity concept, then one is thinking of possibilities open to an agent, what the agent can do or, as Berlin put it, ‘how many doors are open.’ If, by contrast, one thinks of freedom as involving self-direction, which implies the presence of control, one has in mind an exercise-concept of freedom as opposed to an opportunity-concept.
L214: If freedom is an exercise concept, it consists not merely in the possibility of doing certain things, but in actually doing certain things in certain ways—for example, in realizing one’s true self or in acting on the basis of rational and well-informed decisions. MacCallum’s single concept might be criticized as failing to capture this exercise concept of freedom, for it seems to cover only cases in which certain opportunities are open or closed for the agent.
L215: However, this defense of the positive-negative distinction, as coinciding with the distinction between exercise- and opportunity-concepts of freedom, has been challenged by Eric Nelson (2005). As Nelson points out, most of the theorists that are traditionally located in the positive camp, such as Green or Bosanquet, do not distinguish between freedom as the absence of constraints and freedom as the doing or becoming of certain things.
L216: For these theorists, freedom is the absence of any kind of constraint whatsoever on the realization of one’s true self (they adopt a maximally extensive conception of constraints on freedom). The absence of all factors that could prevent the action x is, quite simply, equivalent to the realization of x. In other words, if there really is nothing stopping me from doing x—if I possess all the means to do x, and I have a desire to do x, and no desire, irrational or otherwise, not to do x—then I do x.
L217: If this argument is correct, then MacCallum’s triadic relation accommodates the idea of freedom as an exercise concept.
L218: A second reason for the persistence of the distinction might be thought to lie in the centrality of the relation between freedom and ability. Many people seem to persist in the view that if freedom is about being able to do things, then freedom should be understood as a positive rather than a negative concept. Two limits of this view should be evident from the above discussion. First, freedom as ability fails to capture much of what Berlin had in mind in speaking of positive liberty.
L219: Second, some conceptions that are classified as negative by their proponents, such as Kramer’s trivalent conception (2003) or Garnett’s bivalent ability-based conception (2007; 2022a), identify freedom with ability. To these two limits can be added a third: Christman’s conception of liberty, which is unequivocally positive, is in some respects ability-independent (2025).
L220: A third reason might lie in the importance of the distinction between morally-neutral conceptions and morally committed ones, where the former are classed as negative and the latter as positive: for many theorists in Berlin’s negative camp, a person can be free to perform an action whether it is good or evil, valuable or disvaluable, permissible or impermissible, and they can be unfree to act irrespective of whether the prevention in question is justified or not.
L221: Most positive conceptions, by contrast, take the value or permissibility of actions and/or restrictions to be relevant for ascriptions of freedom and unfreedom. Here too, however, the proposed method of categorization implies some false positives.
L222: Locke, normally regarded as one of the fathers of classical liberalism and as a proponent of negative freedom, states that to be at liberty is ‘to be free from restraint and violence from others.’ Yet he also insists that liberty must not be confused with ‘licence’, and ‘that ill deserves the name of confinement which hedges us in only from bogs and precipices’ (Second Treatise, parags. 6 and 57).
L223: While Locke gives an account of constraints on freedom that Berlin would call negative, he seems to endorse an account of MacCallum’s third variable (the things one is free or unfree to do or become) that Berlin would call positive, restricting it to actions that are not immoral (liberty is not licence) and to those that are in the agent’s own interests (I am not unfree if prevented from falling into a bog).
L224: Many contemporary liberals and libertarians have endorsed similarly morally-laden definitions of freedom (Nozick 1974; Rothbard 1982; Bader 2018).
L225: What these difficulties suggest is that the distinction between negative and positive liberty does not correspond to a single clear conceptual divide. But this is not to deny that the distinction continues to have a meaning. Positive and negative conceptions are best understood as stances taken up within intellectual traditions that have developed historically in response to distinct philosophical and political pressures (Garnett 2022b).
L226: In particular, the distinction functions as a rough categorization of different ideas of freedom in terms of the degree of critical distance implied between their proponents and the classical liberal tradition. One way of making sense of this degree of critical distance is by reference to the theorist’s degree of concern with the notion of the self.
L227: Those on the positive side see questions about the nature and sources of a person’s beliefs, desires, and values as relevant to questions about that person’s freedom, whereas those on the negative side, being more faithful to the classical liberal tradition, tend to consider the raising of such questions as in some way indicating a propensity to violate the agent’s dignity or integrity.
L228: One side takes a positive interest in the agent’s beliefs, desires, and values, while the other recommends that we avoid doing so. This thought explains why the label ‘pure negative freedom’ is an uncontroversial one, even though political philosophers diverge widely in how they assess the liberal normative theories that build on its value. Pure negative conceptions completely divorce questions about a person’s freedom from questions about their will.
L229: Impure negative conceptions make some assumptions about typical or rational desires in order not to limit the relevant constraint-types to physical impossibility. Positive conceptions go further, problematizing the will of the agent as part of an endeavour to construct freedom-based critical social and political theories.

L221: Most positive conceptions, by contrast, take the value or permissibility of actions and/or restrictions to be relevant for ascriptions of freedom and unfreedom. Here too, however, the proposed method of categorization implies some false positives.
L222: Locke, normally regarded as one of the fathers of classical liberalism and as a proponent of negative freedom, states that to be at liberty is ‘to be free from restraint and violence from others.’ Yet he also insists that liberty must not be confused with ‘licence’, and ‘that ill deserves the name of confinement which hedges us in only from bogs and precipices’ (Second Treatise, parags. 6 and 57).
L223: While Locke gives an account of constraints on freedom that Berlin would call negative, he seems to endorse an account of MacCallum’s third variable (the things one is free or unfree to do or become) that Berlin would call positive, restricting it to actions that are not immoral (liberty is not licence) and to those that are in the agent’s own interests (I am not unfree if prevented from falling into a bog).
L224: Many contemporary liberals and libertarians have endorsed similarly morally-laden definitions of freedom (Nozick 1974; Rothbard 1982; Bader 2018).
L225: What these difficulties suggest is that the distinction between negative and positive liberty does not correspond to a single clear conceptual divide. But this is not to deny that the distinction continues to have a meaning. Positive and negative conceptions are best understood as stances taken up within intellectual traditions that have developed historically in response to distinct philosophical and political pressures (Garnett 2022b).
L226: In particular, the distinction functions as a rough categorization of different ideas of freedom in terms of the degree of critical distance implied between their proponents and the classical liberal tradition. One way of making sense of this degree of critical distance is by reference to the theorist’s degree of concern with the notion of the self.
L227: Those on the positive side see questions about the nature and sources of a person’s beliefs, desires, and values as relevant to questions about that person’s freedom, whereas those on the negative side, being more faithful to the classical liberal tradition, tend to consider the raising of such questions as in some way indicating a propensity to violate the agent’s dignity or integrity.
L228: One side takes a positive interest in the agent’s beliefs, desires, and values, while the other recommends that we avoid doing so. This thought explains why the label ‘pure negative freedom’ is an uncontroversial one, even though political philosophers diverge widely in how they assess the liberal normative theories that build on its value. Pure negative conceptions completely divorce questions about a person’s freedom from questions about their will.
L229: Impure negative conceptions make some assumptions about typical or rational desires in order not to limit the relevant constraint-types to physical impossibility. Positive conceptions go further, problematizing the will of the agent as part of an endeavour to construct freedom-based critical social and political theories.

L221: Most positive conceptions, by contrast, take the value or permissibility of actions and/or restrictions to be relevant for ascriptions of freedom and unfreedom. Here too, however, the proposed method of categorization implies some false positives.
L222: Locke, normally regarded as one of the fathers of classical liberalism and as a proponent of negative freedom, states that to be at liberty is ‘to be free from restraint and violence from others.’ Yet he also insists that liberty must not be confused with ‘licence’, and ‘that ill deserves the name of confinement which hedges us in only from bogs and precipices’ (Second Treatise, parags. 6 and 57).
L223: While Locke gives an account of constraints on freedom that Berlin would call negative, he seems to endorse an account of MacCallum’s third variable (the things one is free or unfree to do or become) that Berlin would call positive, restricting it to actions that are not immoral (liberty is not licence) and to those that are in the agent’s own interests (I am not unfree if prevented from falling into a bog).
L224: Many contemporary liberals and libertarians have endorsed similarly morally-laden definitions of freedom (Nozick 1974; Rothbard 1982; Bader 2018).
L225: What these difficulties suggest is that the distinction between negative and positive liberty does not correspond to a single clear conceptual divide. But this is not to deny that the distinction continues to have a meaning. Positive and negative conceptions are best understood as stances taken up within intellectual traditions that have developed historically in response to distinct philosophical and political pressures (Garnett 2022b).
L226: In particular, the distinction functions as a rough categorization of different ideas of freedom in terms of the degree of critical distance implied between their proponents and the classical liberal tradition. One way of making sense of this degree of critical distance is by reference to the theorist’s degree of concern with the notion of the self.
L227: Those on the positive side see questions about the nature and sources of a person’s beliefs, desires, and values as relevant to questions about that person’s freedom, whereas those on the negative side, being more faithful to the classical liberal tradition, tend to consider the raising of such questions as in some way indicating a propensity to violate the agent’s dignity or integrity.
L228: One side takes a positive interest in the agent’s beliefs, desires, and values, while the other recommends that we avoid doing so. This thought explains why the label ‘pure negative freedom’ is an uncontroversial one, even though political philosophers diverge widely in how they assess the liberal normative theories that build on its value. Pure negative conceptions completely divorce questions about a person’s freedom from questions about their will.
L229: Impure negative conceptions make some assumptions about typical or rational desires in order not to limit the relevant constraint-types to physical impossibility. Positive conceptions go further, problematizing the will of the agent as part of an endeavour to construct freedom-based critical social and political theories.
