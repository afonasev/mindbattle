One such technology currently being investigated by the NGS Remote Sensing Research and Development Team is Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR).
L4: LIDAR is an active remote sensing system that can be operated in either a profiling or scanning mode using pulses of light to illuminate the terrain.  LIDAR data collection involves mounting an airborne laser scanning system onboard an aircraft along with a kinematic Global Positioning System (GPS) receiver to locate an x, y, z position and an inertial navigation system to monitor the pitch, roll, and heading of the aircraft.
L5: By accurately measuring the round trip travel time of the laser pulse from the aircraft to the ground, a highly accurate spot elevation can be calculated. Depending upon the altitude and speed of the aircraft along with the laser repetition rate it is possible to obtain point densities that would likely take months to collect using traditional ground survey methods.
L6: LIDAR has been tested in a wide variety of applications including assessing post storm damage to beaches, mapping the Greenland ice sheet, and measuring heights within forest timber stands. NGS is examining the possibility of implementing LIDAR into the production of cite5†shoreline manuscripts and cite6†airspace obstruction charts .

L9@P0: our understanding of medieval cartography and sense of place and still has relevance to all peoples in

L29@P0: 3.1 Name and identification details of the items being nominated:
L30@P0: The Hereford Mappa Mundi
L31@P0: 3.2 Description
L32@P0: The Hereford Mappa Mundi is a medieval map of the world, measuring approximately 1626mm x
L33@P0: 1346mm and made up of one sheet of vellum. It has been dated on art historical, historical and
L34@P0: palaeographical grounds to c. 1290-c1310 AD. The map was probably produced at either Lincoln or
L35@P0: Hereford, an inscription names ‘Richard of Haldingham or Lafford’ as its maker but it is not clear who
L36@P0: he was or if he created the Hereford map or its exemplar. The text is written in red and black ink in an
L37@P0: English gothic bookhand. On the map are around 500 illustrations including about 420 views or
L38@P0: symbols of cities and towns, 15 depictions of biblical events, 33 depictions of plants, animals, birds
L39@P0: and fish, 32 pictures of the peoples of the earth and 5 images relating stories from classical mythology.
L40@P0: The illustrations on the map are one of its most remarkable features, both in their variety and number.
L41@P0-1: 1Outside the boundary of the earth at the top the map depicts Christ in judgement and has the letters
L42@P1: m,o,r,s (spelling ‘mors’ – Latin for death) around the outside, this reflects the Christian view that all
L43@P1: earthly life is subject to death and judgement and the map is, above all, a depiction the place of
L44@P1: humankind in the eternal, divinely-ordained order.
L45@P1: The Hereford Mappa Mundi is the only complete survival of a genre of large world maps intended for
L46@P1: display which were once more common. Other examples known to have existed include the Ebstorf
L47@P1: map (destroyed in 1943) and the Duchy of Cornwall map, which is roughly contemporary with the
L48@P1: Hereford Mappa Mundi but of which only a small fragment survives.
L49@P1: It is different from other existing large medieval world maps such as the ‘Catalan world map’, now in
L50@P1: the Bibliothèque Nationale de France, of about 1375 as these are based on ‘portolan charts’ which
L51@P1: depict coastlines more realistically and did not have the spiritual and didactic purpose of the mappae
L52@P1: mundi . In this respect the Hereford Mappa Mundi is our best surviving graphical representation of the
L53@P1: synthesis of classical knowledge with the medieval worldview.
L54@P1: The Hereford Mappa Mundi is world renowned as the most famous extant medieval map. Records of
L55@P1: it having been studied date back to the seventeenth century and it continues to be a source of artistic
L56@P1: inspiration and fascination to the present day. As the only surviving complete example of its type it is
L57@P1: unique and as a manuscript and physical object irreplaceable.
L58@P1: 4 JUSTIFICATION FOR INCLUSION/ ASSESSMENT AGAINST CRITERIA
L59@P1: 4.1 Authenticity
L60@P1: The Hereford Mappa Mundi has been dated by experts in palaeography and art history to between
L61@P1: c1290 and c1310 AD. In addition damage to the map matches the location of nails on a wooden frame
L62@P1: in which it was housed, and possibly drawn. The frame has been dated by dendrochronology to c1300
L63@P1: AD.
L64@P1: 4.2 Wsignificance, uniqueness and irreplaceability
L65@P1: World significance – see below 4.3
L66@P1: Uniqueness – as established above the Hereford Mappa Mundi is the only surviving complete example
L67@P1: of a medieval mappa mundi.
L68@P1: Irreplaceability – as a medieval manuscript and unique survival the Hereford Mappa Mundi is both
L69@P1: physically and intellectually irreplaceable.
L70@P1: 4.3 Criteria of (a) time (b) place (c) people (d) subject and theme (e) form and style
L71@P1: (a) Time- the Hereford Mappa Mundi provides a graphic representation of the world as
L72@P1: understood in medieval Europe. It is based on the synthesis of classical and medieval learning
L73@P1: (b) Place – the Hereford Mappa Mundi is essential to our understanding of medieval awareness of
L74@P1: other lands. The area of Africa depicted is that of North Africa, known in classical times and aside
L75@P1: from some cities listed in classical sources it is populated by strange races. Similarly the map shows
L76@P1: the eastern end of the world as the earthly paradise. Examples of knowledge of other real lands include
L77@P1: the inclusion of the Seres people who lived north of the Himalayas and exported silk.
L78@P1: (c) Subject and theme – as the largest and most detailed surviving example of a medieval mappa
L79@P1: mundi the Hereford Mappa Mundi uniquely represents the culmination of a tradition of mapmaking
L80@P1: and knowledge which stretches back to the Roman period. The history of cartography after the
L81@P1: medieval mappae mundi begins to move away from the didactic, spiritual towards an increasingly
L82@P1: accurate depiction of the known world, relying more on seafarers’ explorations and advances in
L83@P1: science. It is vital to our understanding of medieval cartography and of medieval perceptions of the
L84@P1: world.
L85@P1-2: 2(d) Form and style – as stated in 3.2 the Hereford Mappa Mundi is the only complete surviving
L86@P2: example of a large ‘traditional’ mappamundi and has been described by Dr Christopher de Hamel, one
L87@P2: of Britain’s leading experts on medieval manuscripts, as ‘without parallel the most important and
L88@P2: most celebrated medieval map in any form’. Aside from its status as a unique survival it is also one of
L89@P2: the most remarkable of surviving medieval manuscripts and has been described by Dr Christopher de
L90@P2: Hamel as ‘certainly the greatest extant thirteenth century picture’.

L156@P4: Hereford Cathedral – Mappa Mundi Management Plan
L157@P4: Storage
L158@P4: The Hereford Mappa Mundi is kept permanently on display in an environmentally controlled space.
L159@P4: The temperature is set to 17°C and the humidity at 55%RH, the environmental conditions. Light levels
L160@P4: are kept to 25 lux with UV filtering being used on all glass.
L161@P4: Conservation
L162@P4: The Dean and Chapter retain Mr Christopher Clarkson as their conservator and the map is inspected
L163@P4: by him every other year. No treatment of the map is carried out although some work needs to be done
L164@P4: to remove Victorian repairs. Treatment would be carried out if recommended by the conservator, for
L165@P4: instance to remove mould if any were found on the map.
L166@P4: Fire Prevention
L167@P4: The Mappa Mundi Chamber presents a low fire risk. Electric lights are separated from the Hereford
L168@P4: Mappa Mundi and fibre optic cables used to provide light for viewing the map. The case used to
L169@P4: display the map in is made of concrete which will provide some measure of protection from fire.
L170@P4: Emergency Measures
L171@P4: The Cathedral Library has a full disaster plan which encompasses the Hereford Mappa Mundi. Any
L172@P4: emergency will be handled in accordance with the Cathedral Library’s disaster plan and advice taken
L173@P4: immediately from the contracted conservator. A contract to provide assistance in the event of an
L174@P4: emergency is held with Harwell Restoration and Drying who will provide equipment and salvage
L175@P4: expertise.The greatest risk is from fire and in that event the first line of response is to contain the fire
L176@P4: using the inert gas suppression system and fire brigade resources. Given the difficulties of access to
L177@P4: the map and the protection offered by the case it will not normally be removed from its case until the
L178@P4: fire has been contained.
L179@P4: Budget
L180@P4: Funding for the conservation of the Hereford Mappa Mundi is provided by the Hereford Mappa Mundi
L181@P4: Trustees. Currently this is restricted to paying the cost of the conservator’s contract but the trustees
L182@P4: would if necessary fund interventitive conservation work. The running costs for the plant to maintain
L183@P4: the environmental conditions are met by the Dean and Chapter of Hereford.
L184@P4: Access
L185@P4: The map is on permanent display as part of an exhibition which includes material to assist with the
L186@P4: interpretation of the map. Material to assist scholars with research is available in the CathedralLibrary
L187@P4: and Archives. Images of the map are supplied to a wide-range of users from scholars to comercial
L188@P4: organisations.
L189@P4: Known Issues
L190@P4: Access to remove the map is very difficult and places the map at greater risk than is necessary. The
L191@P4: Victorian parchment patches added to the map may be distorting the medieval vellum. The height of
L192@P4: the map from the floor can make viewing difficult for children and the disabled in particular. Extremes
L193@P4: of weather or large numbers of visitors can cause the temperature and humidity to change more than is
L194@P4: desirable (although the effects of the changes on the map are moderated by the case it is housed in).
L195@P4: Future Developments
L196@P4: A feasibility study is currently looking at ways of altering the map’s case to make access for inspection
L197@P4: easier and improve its visibility for all. If possible the Victorian parchment repairs will be removed
L198@P4: and replaced with more sensitive repairs, it is not yet clear if this is possible without posing an undue
L199@P4: risk to the map. Consideration is being given to redeveloping the exhibition to incorporate new
L200@P4: knowledge and improve its interpretation but no funding has been identified for this. It is hoped that a
L201@P4: digital facsimile of the Hereford Mappa Mundi will be produced at some stage and initial
L202@P4: investigations into cost and practicality are underway.
