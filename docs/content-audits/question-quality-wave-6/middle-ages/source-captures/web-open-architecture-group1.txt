L213:   * cite113†Description L214:   * cite114†Significance L215:   * cite115†Research L216:   * cite116†Sources L217: # Description of Castle Acre Castle and Bailey Gate
L218: In its final medieval form, the castle consisted of a high motte or mound at the north end, a large outer bailey to the south and a smaller bailey to the east, all defined by earthwork ramparts crowned with stone walls. The motte enclosed and partly buried the remains of an 11th-century domestic building of unique form, much altered in the 12th century. The adjacent town was surrounded by earthwork defences in the mid-12th century, to which stone gatehouses were added in about 1200.
L219: The Bailey Gate is the surviving example.

L223: ## Castle
L224: 
L225: The castle stands to the east of the town, and formed the town's defences on this side. To the south it borders the river Nar and overlooks the broad valley.
L226: 
L227: The castle we see today is a fine example of the ‘motte and bailey’ format, but in this case it is the result of successive alterations (see ‘Castle Acre Castle – development of the Inner Bailey’, downloadable from the link below).
L228: 
L229: Inner Bailey
L230: Covering about 3 hectares (to the outer edges of its defences), the castle has two main parts. At the north end is the circular mound or motte, carrying the inner bailey, and separated from the rising ground to the north-west and from the outer bailey by deep ditches. Remnants survive of the wall encircling the motte’s summit, of the gatehouse facing the bailey, and of the stone building in the centre (consolidated and left exposed after excavation in 1972–83).
L231: The ruins of the castle’s west gate viewed from the inner bailey. To the right (west), facing the town, are the bases of two semicircular turrets. In the foreground are the remains of the guard room
L232: 
L233: Outer Bailey and Barbican
L234: 
L235: To the south lies the larger outer bailey. Roughly rectangular, this is surrounded by a massive earth bank crowned with surviving stretches of a stone wall, reinforced by ditches.
L236: The main entrance to the bailey was from the town side, defended by a gatehouse (the west gate) similar to the town’s ‘Bailey Gate’ (below) and added in about 1200. The surviving stonework shows that it had a portcullis, massive gates, and a guardroom to one side. It was approached across the ditch (now filled) by a timber bridge.
L237: Fragments of a less elaborate east gate of the same date also survive, also approached by a timber bridge, here partially reconstructed on the basis of archaeological evidence.^{cite118†[1] }
L238: Within the outer bailey, low-relief ridges in the turf mark the layout of a great hall (probably early 13th-century) and other rooms, so far unexcavated.
L239: 
L240: A third fortified enclosure adjoining the castle to the east, known today as the Barbican, reinforced the entrance to the castle on the more vulnerable side, facing open country.
L241: The Bailey Gate seen from Stocks Green. This was the gatehouse guarding the entrance to the fortified town from the north, and was constructed about 1200 as an improvement to the earthwork defences built about 50 years before
L242: ## Bailey Gate
L243: 
L244: In the mid-12th century the settlement at Castle Acre was surrounded, where not protected by the castle, with a massive earth bank and a deep ditch, enclosing a roughly rectangular area of flat ground covering 3.8 hectares. These defences are particularly well preserved on the west side. Property boundaries and the street pattern were probably re-ordered at the same time, aligned on what is now Bailey Street, which ran between the main gates.
L245: In about 1200 new gatehouses were built, one of which is known only from an 18th-century observation.^{cite119†[2] } The other, the Bailey Gate facing Stocks Green, largely survives. It consists of a pointed archway flanked by solid semicircular turrets, behind which the gate-passage could be closed with gates and a portcullis. When built it was flanked by the earthwork rampart (roughly on the line of the existing houses) and approached by a timber bridge across the defensive ditch (now filled in).

L218: 
L219: The castle is dominated by its great cylindrical keep, built in the late 12th century, from which massive, wedge-shaped buttresses project at regular intervals. The inner bailey of the castle is enclosed by a high stone wall, in turn surrounded by massive earthworks that pre-date the stone castle.
L220: 
L221: cite117†Image: Conisbrough Castle from the south, with its magnificent late 12th-century keep on the right L222: Conisbrough Castle from the south, with its magnificent late 12th-century keep on the right
L223: ## Setting
L224: 
L225: Conisbrough Castle occupies a strategic position on a large knoll at the north-east end of the historic town of Conisbrough, which occupies a ridge that rises rather higher, running south-west. The castle’s outer bailey is now roughly defined by Castle Hill to the west, and earthworks to north, east and south.
L226: ## Earthworks
L227: 
L228: The castle earthworks pre-date the 12th-century stone castle, although whether they formed part of the Anglo-Saxon ‘Cyninges burh’, or were built by William de Warenne shortly after the Norman Conquest, is not known.
L229: The knoll on which the castle stands falls away steeply east of the inner bailey. On the other three sides ditches and counterscarps (outer ditch walls) were created to strengthen its position. An earth bank enclosed the inner bailey. This was later replaced by the curtain wall, whose foundations were cut into the bank.
L230: Later, the ground level in the bailey was raised to more or less its present level, between 1 and 2.5 metres above the natural level. The keep, however, was founded on natural ground, which suggests that the earthworks formed a ringwork or, less probably, that there was a small motte on the site of the keep.cite118†^{[1]} L231: 
L232: The remains of the curtain wall on the west side of the inner bailey
L233: ## Curtain Wall
L234: 
L235: The coursed rubble curtain wall surrounding the inner bailey, with quoins (cornerstones) at the angles, is not as well built as the keep. The few openings in the walls may not be original.
L236: To the east, south and west are five small, solid, semicircular mural towers. They are probably contemporary with the wall, though they are not well bonded with it: the south-west tower seems to have leaned outwards at an early date. To the north – where the slope of the hill provides natural defence – the curtain wall has no towers, only shallow pilaster buttresses.
L237: In the south-west wall are the remains of the gatehouse, originally about 8 metres square with solid semicircular towers flanking the entrance. It was partly rebuilt and refaced, probably in the early 14th century.
L238: A substantial barbican – designed to control access to the inner bailey and hinder enemy attack – added further protection to the gatehouse entrance. It was built slightly later than the walls and gatehouse, probably in the mid-13th century. The barbican and gatehouse partly collapsed into the ditch before 1538.cite119†^{[2]} L239: 
L240: An aerial reconstruction of Conisbrough as it may have appeared in about 1440, looking north © Historic England (illustration by Peter Urmston)
L241: ## Inner Bailey Buildings
L242: 
L243: The curtain wall was once lined with buildings on its north, south and west sides, although only the footings and stumps of walls survive. They date from two main phases: the mid- to late 12th century (roughly contemporary with the keep), and the early 14th century.cite120†^{[3]} L244: In the north-west corner was a hall, with service rooms and kitchens to its east. There was a range of buildings against the west wall, probably with a suite of residential chambers at first-floor level above service or storage rooms. The remains of a rectangular building east of the gatehouse may represent a chapel.cite121†^{[4]} L245: 
L246: The keep or great tower of Conisbrough Castle, seen from the south
L247: ## Keep
L248: 
L249: Conisbrough’s 12th-century keep is a massive four-storey limestone cylinder with six wedge-shaped buttresses; rising from a battered plinth, it is built of a local magnesian limestone and faced in ashlar of superb quality. It has circular chambers at four levels, and originally had a fifth storey at wall-walk level. The entrance is at ground-floor level, via modern concrete stairs that replace the original wooden ones.
L250: The vaulted basement room, which contains a well, is only accessible via a circular opening in the vault above. Mural staircases lead from one floor to the next. Both the great chamber on the first floor and the bedchamber above it have a fireplace, a washbasin set into the wall and a latrine in a mural chamber. The second floor also houses a chapel with a rib-vaulted ceiling built within one of the buttresses.
L251: At roof level there was at some point another room surrounded by an enclosed wall passage. Part of its wall survives, with a single door jamb. The six buttresses extended above the battlements as turrets, two of which were solid. The others seem to have functioned as water tanks, a bread oven and a dovecote.

L103: “Then arose new architects who after the manner of their barbarous nations erected buildings in that style which we call Gothic (dei Gotthi).” Florentine historiographer Giorgio Vasari (1511–1574) was the first to label the architecture of preceding centuries “Gothic,” in reference to the cite25†Nordic tribes that overran the Roman empire in the sixth century.
L104: Vasari implied that this architecture was debased, especially compared to that of his own time, which had revived the forms of cite26†classical antiquity . Long since rid of derogatory connotations, the label is now used to characterize an art form based on the pointed arch, which emerged around Paris in the middle of the twelfth century, was practiced throughout Europe, and lingered in some regions well into the sixteenth century.
L105: Gothic architecture is the result of an engineering challenge: how to span in stone ever-wider surfaces from ever-greater heights? While most early medieval churches were covered with timber ceilings, many cite27†Romanesque buildings have either stone barrel vaults (i.e., semi-circular) or groin vaults (i.e., bays of barrel vaults crossing at a right angle). Their walls are necessarily thick to counter the outward thrust of the vault, and they allow only small windows.
L106: From 1100 onward, architects experimented with innovations that, once properly combined, allowed the dissolution of the wall and a fluid arrangement of space. For example, they adopted the pointed arch, which has a lesser lateral thrust than the round arch and is easily adaptable to openings of various widths and heights.
L107: They also developed a system of stone ribs to distribute the weight of the vault onto columns and piers all the way to the ground; the vault could now be made of lighter, thinner stone and the walls opened to accommodate ever-larger windows. Equally important, cite28†flying buttresses†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io began to appear in the 1170s, whose vertical members (uprights) are connected to the exterior wall of the building with bridge-like arches (flyers).
L108: These external structures absorb the outward thrust of the vault at set intervals just under the roof, making it possible to reduce the building’s exterior masonry shell to a mere skeletal framework.
L109: The new architectural grammar was first coherently articulated in the ambulatory (chevet) of the cite29†royal abbey church of Saint-Denis†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io , north of Paris, built under Abbot Suger between 1140 and 1144. Two concentric aisles are separated by cite30†slender columns†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io : the outer aisle is covered by five-part and the inner aisle by four-part rib vaults.
L110: The resulting effect is one of clear spatial distribution and organic lightness: the bays are opened on all sides and the walls of the radiating chapels, no longer load-bearing, have large openings filled with stained glass.
L111: With growing assurance, architects in northern France, and soon all over Europe, competed in a race to conquer height. The vault of each new cathedral strained to surpass that of its predecessors by a few meters. The dramatic collapse in 1284 of the tallest among them, cite31†Beauvais†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io , marked the vertical limits of Gothic architecture.
L112: Its choir and transept were rebuilt soon afterwards to the original 48 meters, now supported by twice as many cite32†flying buttresses†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io .
L113: The typical cite33†elevation of a Gothic cathedral interior†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io , with storey upon corresponding storey, draws the gaze to the highest point in the vault, in an irresistible upward pull symbolic of the Christian hope of leaving the terrestrial world for a heavenly realm. Such a transcendent experience of architecture is reinforced by the rich cite34†stained-glass windows , sometimes spanning the entire height of the edifice.
L114: Decorated with scenes from the Bible, the lives of the saints (Scenes from the Passion of Saint Vincent of Saragossa and the History of His Relics, (24.167a-k)), or with larger figures of prophets and other personages, stained-glass windows were central to the perception of the cathedral as a compendium of the Christian faith.
L115: Throughout the thirteenth century, an obligatory feature in most cathedrals was the cite35†monumental rose-window†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io with God, Christ, or the Virgin at its center surrounded by the cosmos. The shimmering, colored light called to mind the heavenly Jerusalem described in the Book of Revelations (the Apocalypse) as a city of gold and precious stones.
L116: cite36†The Last Judgment often carved on the tympanum of the main portal was a stark reminder of the solemnity of the space the faithful were about to enter. It is on the cite37†west facade of Saint-Denis†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io , around 1140, that portals were first flanked by standing figures, known as jamb statues (Head of King David, (38.180)), a format repeated ever since.
L117: With their insatiable demand for cite38†figurative sculptures†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io to adorn portals, archivolts, tympanums, choir screens (Head of an Angel, (1990.132)) and cite39†foliate capitals†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io for the interior, cathedrals and churches were crucibles of sculptural innovation. Teams of sculptors labored for years on the decoration of a cathedral, before moving to another site, thereby disseminating styles over wide regions.
L118: Some of the sculptors active on the west facade of Reims Cathedral, for example, later contributed to the cite40†sculptural program of Bamberg Cathedral, several hundred miles away. The stylistic language first formulated in stone on a monumental scale resonated in other media.
L119: In their elongated curved pose and enigmatic smile, the wooden altar angels at The Cloisters (52.33.1), and several like them, ultimately derive from their cousins on the cite41†west facade of Reims Cathedral†dev-met-timeline.pantheon.io .
L120: Gothic vocabulary gradually permeated all forms of art throughout Europe. Pointed arches, trefoils, quatrelobes, and other architectural ornaments were adopted on metalwork, such as reliquaries and cite42†liturgical vessels (17.190.360), on rich ecclesiastic vestments (27.162.1), on precious diptychs intended for cite43†private devotion (1980.366); (1970.324.7a,b), on cite44†illuminated manuscripts (1990.217), as well as on secular items such as furniture, combs, or spoons.
L121: Subject to regional and temporal variations, Gothic art shaped human perception in Europe for nearly four centuries.
