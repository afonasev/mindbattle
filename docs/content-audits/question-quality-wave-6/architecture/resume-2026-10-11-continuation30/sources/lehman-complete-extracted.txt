L376: [Button: A ]
L377: abacus:  The flat plate on the top of a capital on which the architrave rests.
L378: acroteria: Decorative pedestal for an ornament or statue placed atop the pediment.
L379: adobe:  Often used to describe an architectural style; adobe is actually a building material made with tightly compacted earth, clay, and straw.
L380: aisle: The portion of a church flanking the nave and separated from it by a row of columns or piers. In general, the space between the arcade and an outer wall.
L381: American Renaissance: In the history of American architecture and the arts, the period ca 1876 - 1914 characterized by renewed national self-confidence and a feeling that the United States was the heir to Greek democracy, Roman law, and Renaissance humanism.
L382: antebellum:  Latin for “before war.”  Refers to elegant plantation homes built in the American South before the Civil War.
L383: ante fixes:  small decorative fixtures put at the eaves of a roof of a classic building to hide the ends of the tiles.
L384: apron:  The lower part of a molded cornice.
L385: apse:  In a church, a semicircular or polygonal projection at the altar (usually east) end, beyond the sanctuary.
L386: arcade:  A series of arches supported by piers or columns.
L387: arch:  A structure, especially one of masonry, forming the curved, pointed, or flat upper edge of an open space and supporting the weight above it, as in a bridge or doorway.
L388: architrave:  A horizontal beam or lintel, that rests on columns or piers; or the lowest portion of an entablature; or a decorative molding around a door, a window, or an arch.
L389: archivolt:  Bands or moldings surrounding an arched opening.
L390: armature: A framework serving as a supporting core.
L391: Art Deco:  The Art Deco style evolved from many sources. The austere shapes of the Bauhaus School and streamlined styling of modern technology combined with patterns and icons taken from the Far East, ancient Greece and Rome, Africa, Egypt, India, and Mayan and Aztec cultures.  Building often styled with zigzag patterns and vertical lines that create dramatic effects.
L392: Art Moderne:  Stripped-down forms and geometric designs, the Art Moderne style appears sleek and plain.  Features are asymmetrical, horizontal orientation, flat roof,
L393: no cornices or eaves ,  cube-like shape, smooth, white walls, sleek, streamlined appearance, and rounded corners highlighted by wraparound windows.
L394: Art Nouveau:  Ornate motifs with strong organic curves.  Highlights:  cross-lined designs, rounded corners and intersecting planes.
L395: Arts & Crafts style:  Stylistic design of interior woodwork with minimalistic design and precise craftsmanship.  English and Dutch origin.
L396: ashlar:  Stone that is cut square.  Masonry using thin slabs of squared stone as facing material.
L397: atrium:  An inner courtyard that is open to the air. The open central courtyard of an ancient Roman house.
L398: attic story: Refers to a story or low wall above the cornice of a classical façade.
L399: axis:  An imaginary line to which elements of a work of art, such as a picture, are referred for measurement or symmetry.
L400: [Button: B ]
L401: baldachin: Decorated canopy, usually over an altar.
L402: balustrade:  A decorative railing composed of post (balusters) and a handrail often used at the front of a parapet or gallery.
L403: bandcourse:  A thin projecting course of brickwork or stone; also “stringcourse.”
L404: Baroque:  Ornate classical motifs and eccentric mathematic architecture accented with bright colors and gold trim.
L405: barrel roof:  Like a covered wagon, or inverted ship; barrel vault is a plain vault of uniform cross section.
L406: bas-relief or low relief:  Sculpture in which the carved forms project only slightly from the background.
L407: bastions: 1. Projecting parts of a fortification. 2. Well fortified positions.
L408: battlement: A notched parapet built on top of a wall.
L409: batten board:  A small strip of wood used to cover joints.
L410: Bauhaus style:  Bauhaus buildings have flat roofs, smooth facades, and cubic shapes. Colors are white, gray, beige, or black. Floor plans are open and furniture is functional. Segmentation of space into modular units showcased in glass, screens and moveable walls.
L411: bay:  Compartment or unit of division of an interior or of a facade - usually between one window or pillar and the next.
L412: Bay window:  A window or set of windows that project out from a wall, forming an alcove or small space in a room.
L413: Beaux-Arts:  Distinguished by its emphasis on the beautiful plan and clear distinction of function.  Coupled columns, monumental flights of stairs, figural sculptures, and both Greek and Roman elements are features.
L414: bell-cote:  A small shelter for bells; has a gable or shed roof.
L415: belvedere:  An open sided structure designed to offer extensive views, normally in a formal garden.
L416: blind arcades: A row of decorative arches that looks like an arcade but is attached to a wall surface and has no real openings.
L417: blind arch:  An arch applied to a wall.
L418: bracketed style: A term occasionally used for the Italianate style. An eclectic style of Italian-influenced residential and commercial architecture; fashionable in England and America from the 1840s to around 1890.
L419: blind opening:  A term applied to windows, arches, balustrades, etc. that are applied to a wall for decorative purposes but are blocked with recessed stone, brick, etc.
L420: board and batten: Describes a type of exterior siding or interior paneling that has alternating wide boards and narrow wooden strips.
L421: boss: In masonry construction, a projecting ornament, often located at the intersection of two components.
L422: brutalist architecture:  Named for “beton brut.”  The French term for concrete with an unfinished surface.
L423: bulls-eye window: Oeil-de boeuf means bulls eye—a small circular window.
L424: buttress:  A structure built against a wall to support or reinforce it. When the buttress is a free-standing pier attached to the wall by one or more arches, it is called a flying buttress.
L425: [Button: C ]
L426: calyxes: The outermost group of floral parts.
L427: campanile:  Detached bell-tower, most commonly found in Italy.
L428: canopy:  An ornamental, roof-like projection or covering.
L429: Cape Cod style:  Originated in colonial New England. The first Cape Cod style homes were built by English colonists who came to America in the late 17th century. They modeled their homes after the half-timbered houses of England, but adapted the style to the stormy New England weather. Over the course of a few generations, a modest, one- to one-and-a-half-story house with wooden shutters emerged.
L430: cantilever:  A horizontal platform extending from a building, such as a step, balcony, beam or canopy that is without external bracing and appears to be self-supporting.
L431: capital:  A decorative form placed at the top of a column.
L432: caryatid:  Sculptured female figure  serving as a supporting column.
L433: cartouche:  An oval tablet with an elaborate scroll-carved frame, used as ornamentation for building moldings, borders, panels, etc.
L434: cast-in-place concrete: Cement mixture that is deposited as plastic concrete, which will harden as part of the structure, as opposed to pre-cast concrete.
L435: casemates: fortified enclosure for artillery on a warship.
L436: casement window: A hinged window that swings open to one side.
L437: castellated:  Decorated with battlements or a serrated edge (a wall with indentation and raised portions that alternate; also known as crenellation.  Resembling the top of a castle wall.
L438: chair rail:  A molding that surrounds a room at chair back height to prevent scuffing and damage to walls.
L439: chancel:  The area around the altar of a church for the clergy and choir; often enclosed by a lattice or railing.
L440: chateau:  A manor house or residence of the lord of the manor or a country house of nobility or gentry , with or without fortifications, originally - and still most frequently - in French -speaking regions.
L441: clapboard:  Similar in appearance to roofing shingles, clapboard is wooden boards that overlap each other on the side of a house.
L442: cladding:  A term used to describe the siding or materials covering the exterior of a building.
L443: clerestory: A row of windows located near the top of the wall of a nave  of a church or room or other space.
L444: coffer:  A decorative panel that is recessed in a ceiling, vault, or dome.
L445: Colonial Revival style:  Three major types of Colonial Revival styles are Neo-Georgian, Neo-Adamesque, and Dutch Colonial Revival.  Highlights are front door with pediment; double-hung, multi paned windows; classical details; single side-gabled roof.
L446: colonnade:  Columns in a row that form an architectural composition, beneath either a flat-topped entablature or a row of arches.
L447: column:  A slender, upright structure, usually a supporting member in a building. Usually with a capital at the top and a base at the bottom.
L448: concave: Curving inward.
L449: conical:  Having the form of, resembling, or pertaining to a cone.
L450: console: A type of bracket or corbel, particularly one with a scroll-shaped profile.
L451: Continental style: Of or relating to the mainland of Europe; European.
L452: convex: Having a surface that is curved or rounded outward.
L453: coping:  Consists of the capping or covering of a wall.
L454: corbel:  A decorative device often used for pilasters, mantels, or load bearing shelf support.  A bracket of brick or stone that juts out of a wall to support a structure above it.
L455: Corinthian column:  A column decorated at the top with curlicues, scrolls, acanthus leaves, and other extravagant ornamentation.
L456: cornice:  Decorative ledge along top of wall.  The top projecting section of the part of a classical building that is supported by the columns (entablature).
L457: Cotswold Cottage style: With roots in the pastoral Cotswold region of England, the picturesque Cotswold Cottage style is reminiscent of a cozy storybook house.  Features include sloping, uneven roof; brick, stone or stucco siding; prominent brick or stone chimney; small dormer windows; low doors and arched doors; and sloping walls in rooms on upper floor.
L458: course: Level layer of stones or bricks.
L459: Craftsman and Bungalow style:  Based on the Arts and Crafts movement, the bungalow represented a simple, efficient, house, with a front porch that encouraged outdoor living.  Highlights of Craftsman and Bungalow style are: low-pitched gable roof; wide overhands; exposed roof rafters and wood structure; porch; tapered square column supports.
L460: crenellation:  A series of gaps in the low wall at the edge of a roof.
L461: cross gabled: Roof having additional sections or wings crossing perpendicular to the main section, meeting in a valley, each with its own peaked or gabled façade.
L462: cross-timbering: A building with exposed wood framing. The spaces between the wooden timbers are filled with plaster, brick, or stone. Used in early building.
L463: crown molding:  Where the wall meets the ceiling to hide uneven surfaces and provides visual interest.
L464: cruciform:  Shaped like a cross.
L465: cupola: A small dome on a tower or a roof.
L466: curtain wall: A lightweight exterior cladding that is hung on the building structure, usually from floor to floor.
L467: cylindrical: Having the shape of a cylinder, especially of a circular cylinder.
L468: [Button: D ]
L469: dado:  Lower part of an interior wall when paneled or painted separately from the main part.
L470: dentil:  A decorative feature in a cornice consisting of a row of small, tooth-like square blocks.
L471: diaper: A pattern formed by small, repeated geometrical motifs set adjacent to one another, used to decorate stone surfaces.
L472: dome: A roof or vault, usually hemispherical in form.
L473: Doric column:  A Greek-style column with a smooth or slightly rounded band of wood, stone or plaster as a simple decoration around the top. The simplest of the three classical orders of Greek architecture.
L474: dormer: A window that projects from a sloping roof.
L475: Duranodic: Paint color that is an industry standard.
L476: Dutch Colonial style: A style of American domestic architecture, primarily characterized by gambrel roofs having curved eaves along the length of the house. Central to the style is a broad gambrel roof with flaring eaves that extend over the long sides, resembling a barn in construction.
L477: Earlier homes were a single room, with additions added to either end (or short side), and very often a porch along both long sides. Typically, end walls were made of stone and a chimney was located on one or both ends. Common were double-hung sash windows with outward swinging wood casements and a central double Dutch door .
L478: [Button: E ]
L479: Early Colonial style: Sometimes referred to as "first period". Not really a specific style, but rather the form created by function by the earliest settlers. Typically, most had steep roofs and smaller windows.
L480: eave:  The projecting lower edge of a roof.
L481: eaves courses: The lowest course of tiles or slates.
L482: egg and dart molding: Molding in which an egg shape alternates with a dart shape. Classical ornamental design that forms a course of alternating oval shapes and arrows.
L483: Egyptian period: A stepped stone system.  Royal tomb pyramids and temples constructed of layered cut stone block or hollowed out rock tombs.  Columned or pillared halls, porticos (entryways) and sacred chambers embellished with historical or royal inscriptions and relief sculptures.
L484: elevation: Drawing of an exterior of a structure.
L485: Elizabethan style: The term given to early Renaissance architecture in England, during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I.  Stylistically it followed Tudor architecture and was succeeded in the 17th century by Palladian architecture introduced by Inigo Jones.  In England the Renaissance tended to manifest itself in large square tall houses (like small castles), often with symmetrical towers which hint at the evolution from medieval fortified architecture.
L486: embrasures:  An opening in a wall that frames a doorway or a window that which allows guns to be fired from.  Masonry buildings in the Gothic Revival style may have architectural decoration that resembles battlements.
L487: encaustic tiles:  Ceramic tiles in which the pattern or figure on the surface is not a product of the glaze but of different colors of clay. They are usually of two colors but a tile may be composed of as many as six. The pattern is inlaid into the body of the tile, so as the tile is worn down the design remains.
L488: engaged (or applied) column:  A column that is attached to the wall so that only half of the form projects from the wall.
L489: English Collegiate Gothic: Derives from Gothic Revival, an architectural style inspired by medieval Gothic architecture. The Gothic style was promoted by architect Ralph Adams Cram for college campuses of the early 20th century. Features include:  asymmetrical front façade; pointed arch doorways; stone lace-like ornamentation; and statuary niches.
L490: entablature:  This often decorated, horizontal component lies between the columns and the roof; in classical architecture, the entablature consists of an architrave, a frieze, and a cornice. escutcheon:  Shield or shield-shaped surface on which a coat of arms is depicted.
L491: [Button: F ]
L492: façade:  An exterior wall of a building.  The principal or front face of a building showing its most prominent architectural features.
L493: fan light:  Glazed area above a doorway, designed to brighten the hallway inside.  A type of transom.
L494: fan vaulting: A method of vaulting employed in the Perpendicular style, so called because of its resemblance to a fan. The ribs radiate from one point with the same curvature, and are equidistant. The intermediate spaces between the ribs are generally filled in with smaller ribs and with decorative ornaments that sometimes give it the name of ‘fan-tracery’.
L495: Federal / Adam Style: Graceful details distinguish these homes from the pragmatic Georgian colonial style. Highlights include: low pitched roof; smooth façade; semi-circular fanlight over entry door; and geometric forms.
L496: fenestration:  The stylistic arrangement of windows in a building.
L497: Flamboyant style: The final development in French Gothic architecture that reached its height in the 15th century. It is characterized chiefly by ornate tracery forms that, by their suggestion of flames, gave the style its name. Flamboyant works exhibit pronounced freedom and exuberance, created by high, attenuated proportions, accumulated and elaborate traceries, and many crockets, pinnacles, and canopied niches.
L498: fleche:  A slender spire, especially one on a church above the intersection of the nave and transepts.  The word is French for “arrow.”
L499: flute (or fluting):  Shallow, concave grooves running vertically on a column, pilaster, or other surface.
L500: Fieldstone:  A stone used in its natural shape.
L501: finial:  An ornamental projection at the top of a gable, spire, arched structure, or roof.
L502: Fish scale Shingles  A shingle having straight sides and rounded bottoms.
L503: floor plan: A simple line drawing showing rooms as if seen from above. Walls, doorways, and windows are often drawn to scale.  A complete set of construction plans will also contain many other types of diagrams, such as cross-section drawings, electrical plans, and elevation drawings.
L504: fluting: Shallow, concave grooves running vertically on a column, pilaster, or other surface.
L505: flying buttress:   A buttress arched over at the top to engage with a main wall.  A principal feature of Gothic architecture, lending strength and solidity to the main structure.
L506: flying stair: Cantilevered from the walls of a stairwell, without newels; sometimes called a Geometric stair when the inner edge describes a curve.
L507: formalism: An emphasis on form and visual relationships between the building parts and the work as a whole. Shape, often on a monumental scale, is the focus of attention. Lines and rigid geometric shapes predominate in Formalist architecture. Formalism found in many Modernist buildings, especially in Bauhaus and International Style architecture.
L508: foundation:  The base of a house providing stability.
L509: foyer:  The entrance hall or vestibule of a home.
L510: frame:  Of wood construction.
L511: French Renaissance: In France in the 16th century Renaissance taste made one of its first tentative appearances in the Louis XII wing of the château of Blois. In the first period Gothic traditions persisted in plan, structure, and exterior masses, onto which fresh and graceful Renaissance details were grafted.
