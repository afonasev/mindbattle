Crystal Graphics Research: The Structure of Individual Crystals

The Structure of Individual Crystals

A crystal aggregate is a collection of crystals. Thus, even though the crystals in an aggregate aren’t necessarily ideal, or even distinct, understanding how to mathematically and algorithmically model individual crystals is a reasonable place to start the study of aggregates. Our work being ultimately directed to the appearance of crystals, their symmetry is a key property we need to capture. Crystallography has a rich nomenclature for categorizing and describing crystal symmetry, and mathematics has powerful tools for describing and discussing symmetry. This page explains crystallography’s description of symmetry and explores how it relates to mathematical descriptions. The overarching question is whether a crystal’s symmetry distills into a few pieces of primitive information from which its complete geometry follows mathematically and algorithmically.

Symmetry Operations

Symmetry in crystals is described by the mathematical operations of rotation about an axis, reflection across a plane, and inversion through a point applied to vertices, edges, or faces of the crystal. In particular, applying one of these operations to a vertex, edge, or face of a crystal produces another vertex, edge, or face of the same crystal, and relative to this new vertex, edge, or face the crystal “looks” the same as it did relative to the original one. Operations that preserve appearance in this way are called symmetry operations. However, not every rotation, reflection, or inversion that can be defined mathematically is a symmetry operation for a physically realizable crystal; the actual symmetry operations for a crystal follow from its crystal system.

Mathematically, the symmetry operations for crystals form algebraic groups. In algebra, a group is a set of values with an associative operator that acts on those values; the set of values is closed with respect to the operator, includes an identity element for the operator, and every value in the set also has an inverse in the set. The (positive and negative) integers with addition is an example of a group. Pertinent to crystals, symmetry operations can also form groups, with the operations being the values and function composition being the operator. For now, we are concerned only with point groups, i.e., groups in which every symmetry operation maps at least one point to itself.

For more information on crystal symmetry operations, see crystallography texts such as O’Keefe et al. 1996 or Megaw 1973.

Rotations, reflections, and inversions have well-known matrix forms, which can be found in most linear algebra texts (or can easily be derived). To apply an operation represented by matrix \(M\) to point \(p = (x, y, z)\), compute the product \(M \vec{p}\) where vector \(\vec{p}\) is point \(p\) treated as a column vector. Typical matrix forms for crystal symmetry operations include…

Inversion through the Origin

This symmetry transforms point \((x, y, z)\) to point \((-x, -y, -z)\). Its matrix form is simply

\[\begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\]

Mirror Plane

This symmetry transforms point \((x, y, z)\) to its reflection across some plane. For example, considering the mirror plane \(z = 0\), the transformation maps point \((x, y, z)\)to \((x, y, -z)\). The matrix form for such a reflection about the \(z = 0\) plane is

\[\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\]

Rotation Axes

Rotational symmetry in crystals always involves rotation through an angle whose measure in degrees is of the form \( \tfrac{360}{n}\), for \(n = 2, 3, 4\), or 6. These symmetries are n-fold, meaning that applying the rotation once, twice (i.e., rotating the result of the rotation), three times, and so forth up to \(n-1\) times are all symmetry operations (applying the rotation \(n\) times is the identity operation). Axes of rotational symmetry for \(n = 2, 3, 4\), and 6 are sometimes called diad, triad, tetrad, and hexad axes, respectively. The matrix form for a rotation through angle \(\Theta\); counterclockwise about the \(z\) axis is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Theta & -\sin\Theta & 0 \\ \sin\Theta & \cos\Theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\]

Rotoinversion

This is a rotation followed by an inversion, i.e., a “turn + flip.” Its matrix form is therefore simply the product of an inversion and a rotation. For example, rotating about the z axis and inverting through the origin again…

\[ \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\cos\Theta & -\sin\Theta & 0 \\ \sin\Theta & \cos\Theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\cos\Theta & \sin\Theta & 0 \\ -\sin\Theta & -\cos\Theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} \]

The above examples give a sense for what crystal symmetry operations look like for certain convenient points, planes, or axes, but they are not a complete catalog. In actual practice, one would either use similar transformations derived for a different axis (e.g., if the relevant axis were the \(x\) or \(y\) axis rather than the \(z\)), or would first transform points in a crystal’s coordinate system to a coordinate system in which the above transformations can be used, apply the transformation, and finally transform back into the crystal system (e.g., if the symmetry axis were a diagonal or edge of the crystal).

Notation for Symmetry Elements

A concise notation exists to describe symmetries about an axis through a point (adapted from Neese, 2000):

  • If the symmetry is n-fold rotation about the axis, simply write \(n\) as a single digit, i.e., 2, 3, 4, or 6.
  • As an extension of this notation, a complete absence of symmetry can be denoted by the digit 1 (i.e., the only way to transform points on the object into “symmetrical” points on the same object is to rotate 360 degrees).
  • If the axis is in fact the normal to a mirror plane through the point, write the letter m.
  • A rotation axis that is also normal to a mirror plane is notated as n/m, where \(n\) is the degree of rotational symmetry. For example, 2/m, 4/m.
  • Rotoinversion is indicated by a number with a bar over it (sometimes pronounced bar-n where \(n\) is the number), the number being the degree of rotational symmetry. For example \(\overline{2}, \overline{3}, \overline{4}, \overline{6}\)
  • As a special case, bar-1 denotes simple inversion through the point.

Hermann-Mauguin notation is an extension of these conventions to describe symmetries with respect to multiple axes. Specifically, Hermann-Mauguin notation joins symbols from the above list into strings, one symbol per axis (or group of axes equivalent in terms of symmetry behavior). For example, the Hermann-Mauguin description of a system with two symmetry axes, the first of which is a diad axis with a mirror plane and the second of which is a triad rotoinversion axis is \(2\mathrm{/m}\overline{3}\).

See any crystallography text (e.g., Megaw, 1973) for more information on Hermann-Mauguin notation.

Crystal Systems and Classes

The symmetries present in crystals are described by a two-level hierarchy. The top level consists of six crystal systems, defined by the relationships between the natural axes for describing the crystal (its crystallographic axes). These relationships in turn give rise to a characteristic symmetry for the system. Crystal systems are further refined into crystal classes, whose members display additional symmetries beyond the characteristic one of the system as a whole. For more on crystal systems and classes, see (Megaw, 1973; Neese, 2000).

Crystallographic axes are not necessarily simple Cartesian coordinate systems. In particular, the unit distance for each axis (loosely called the axis’s “length”) may be different along different axes, and axes needn’t be mutually perpendicular. Crystallographers commonly use the letters \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) to identify crystallographic axes. Axes that are interchangeable, e.g., because they have the same length and angular relationships to other axes, may be labeled with a common letter and a subscript, e.g., \(a_1\), \(a_2\). The same labels are used for the lengths of the axes, leaving readers to tell from context whether a letter stands for a length or an axis. Angles between axes are identified by Greek letters \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\) (alpha, beta, and gamma), as follows:

  • Angle between \(a\) and \(b = \gamma\)
  • Angle between \(a\) and \(c = \beta\)
  • Angle between \(b\) and \(c = \alpha\)

As with axes and their lengths, a Greek letter may mean either an angle or its measure, depending on context.

Crystal Forms

A crystal form is a group of faces that can be generated from each other by the crystal’s symmetry. Unfortunately, a form is not always a complete description of a crystal shape. Crystals often contain several forms, and there are even forms — so-called open forms — that don’t completely enclose a volume of space. Forms that do fully enclose a volume are called closed forms. For more on crystal forms, see (Neese, 2000).

Shapes in which crystals or groups of crystals commonly occur in nature are called crystal habits. Crystal habits tend to look different from ideal crystal forms, although they may be distortions of ideal forms. For a summary of some common habits and their names, see (Plante, 2003).

The fact that forms don’t correspond to complete crystal shapes is another reason why simple ways of generating crystals from compact descriptions may not exist: while applying a crystal’s symmetry operations, relative to the right axes, to a single face may generate all the other faces in one form, those faces are not necessarily the whole crystal. One needs information about every form present in a crystal to generate the complete crystal.

Some terms used to describe forms include the following (from Neese, 2000):

  • Pinacoid — Two parallel faces on opposite sides of a crystal. The two faces are related by either a mirror or inversion. Open.
  • Prism — A collection of 3, 4, 6, 8, or 12 faces that intersect at parallel edges to form a tube. Faces that close the ends are not part of the form. Cross sections at the base determine the prism’s specific name. Open.
  • Dipyramid — Two pyramids, one being the reflection of the other across a mirror plane, for a total of 6, 8, 12, 16, or 24 faces. Exact name depends on the cross section of the pyramids.

Because form depends on symmetry, which in turn depends on crystal system, there is a connection between crystal system and the forms that crystals in that system can assume. The following table (adapted from Bloss, 1985) lists the possibilities:

Lattice Structures and Unit Cells

Symmetry in crystals arises from the fact that the atoms composing the crystal lie in a lattice. (Megaw, 1973) gives a good presentation of crystal lattices, on which the following is based.

A lattice is a set of points, defined by an origin and a set of 3 basis vectors i, j, k; the lattice is exactly those points whose position relative to the origin is of the form a i + b j + c k where a, b, and c are all integers. In crystals, each point in a lattice is a reference point relative to which one repeating unit of the crystal’s atoms is positioned. Note, however, that atoms don’t have to lie on lattice points, and some atoms can be shared between repeating units. The combination of a lattice and a set of atoms positioned relative to it is called a lattice complex.

Lattices exhibit symmetry. At the very least, for any lattice point a i + b j + c k, -a i - b j - c k is also a lattice point, and so the lattice has inversion symmetry. Since any lattice point can be chosen as the origin, the lattice in fact exhibits inversion symmetry about each lattice point. Lattices may also have rotational symmetry and mirror symmetry, although only 2-, 3-, 4-, and 6-fold rotational symmetries respect the regular spacing of lattice points.

A unit cell is a block within a lattice whose edges are the lattice’s basis vectors. Put more formally, this means that diagonally opposite corners of a unit cell have positions a i + b j + c k and (a+1)i + (b+1)j + (c+1)k. The choice of basis vectors for the lattice thus determines what the unit cells look like and many different choices are possible. Crystallographic conventions favor certain choices of basis over others, for example shorter rather than longer basis vectors, or basis vectors that meet at less rather than more oblique angles. Crystallographers classify unit cells according to whether the basis vectors they imply are or are not of equal length, the angles between the basis vectors, and whether the cells are centered or not (geometrically, centering means that some lattice points are inside unit cells). Because of the limited set of symmetry operators that can apply in lattices, 3-dimensional lattices only exhibit 14 distinct classes of unit cell. There are only 6 distinct combinations of axis length and angle relationships, corresponding exactly to the six crystal systems described elsewhere.

Unit cells define crystallographic axes. Specifically, each crystallographic axis is parallel to a basis vector, and its length (i.e, unit distance) is the length of that basis vector (in arbitrary units). As a result, the angles between crystallographic axes are the angles between basis vectors.

Miller Indices

A Miller index is a sequence of numbers that describes the orientation of a crystal face. Specifically, consider a face and the set of planes parallel to it. An empirical property of crystals, known as "the law of rational indices," says that one of these planes will intersect the crystallographic axes at points (1/h,0,0), (0,1/k,0) and (0,0,1/l), where h, k, and l are small integers with no common factors. If the face is parallel to an axis, the corresponding h, k, or l is taken to be 0. Trusting that h, k, and l are small enough to be written in one digit, the symbol (hkl) is the face’s Miller index.

Miller indices encode normals to faces, as follows: the plane that intersects the axes at (1/h,0,0), (0,1/k,0) and (0,0,1/l) has a plane equation of the form

\[Ax + By + Cz = 1\]

for suitable values of \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\); a standard result in analytic geometry says that vector \((A, B, C)\) is normal to the plane. Plugging the plane’s intersections with the axes into this equation gives

A × 1/h = 1, i.e., A = h

B × 1/k = 1, i.e., B = k

C × 1/l = 1, i.e., C = l

Since (A,B,C) = (h,k,l) is normal to the plane, and the plane is parallel to the face, (h,k,l) is a normal for the face. Beware though that “normal” doesn’t necessarily mean "visually perpendicular" in a coordinate frame whose axes don’t have to be perpendicular or of equal length.

Normals to faces are good things to perform symmetry operations on. Specifically, applying a symmetry operation to a face’s normal gives the normal to the symmetrically related face. For this reason, Miller indices are also used to describe crystal forms: the symbol {hkl} denotes the form containing the faces derived by symmetry from the face with Miller index (hkl). Transforming normals taken from Miller indices thus seems a promising way to generate forms, if not entire crystals.

For more information on Miller indices, see crystallography texts such as (Megaw, 1973; O'Keefe et al., 1996).

Note on Transforming Normals

When representing transformations as matrices and using matrix-vector multiplication to apply transformations, one cannot apply transformation T to normal n by computing the product Tn. A standard result from computer graphics is that the product that produces the correctly transformed normal is (T-1)Tn, i.e., multiply by the transpose of the inverse of the regular transformation matrix.

Conclusion

From the ideas discussed above, the shape of a crystal depends on two things: the forms it exhibits and its class. Crystal class in turn encapsulates two additional kinds of information: what symmetries the crystal has, and what axes, points, or planes they are relative to. On the one hand it should therefore be possible to write software functions that draw arbitrary crystals but still have short parameter lists (e.g., two parameters, a class identifier and a list of forms, should be sufficient). However, at a deeper level, the information actually needed to specify the crystal’s shape seems inherently pretty bulky: a set of symmetry operations, a set of axes, and a set of forms. Furthermore, each of these items has its own internal information, e.g., an axis contains information about origin and direction, a form contains information about at least one plane, etc. It therefore seems that individual crystals do not have as elegantly compact a mathematical description as we initially hoped.
