## Middle Ages

**Castle.** Historic England’s guide supports the broad prompt/key definition: medieval castles were residences of seigneurial families with features expressive of defence. Its p.12 lines 653–660 also say that some sites from the late fourteenth century onward had defensive-looking features plausibly intended for visual effect rather than serious assault. That carefully scoped point supports the updated correct note and explanation. Palace, monastery and cathedral notes describe distinct, sourced institutions. The question is easy, grammatical, and distinct from the palace question. All six fields PASS.

**Palace.** Historic England defines royal palaces as residential complexes for a sovereign’s household, with an administrative purpose and high-quality architecture. It says they may be enclosed by walls but had little defensive capacity, so the prompt’s “without required fortifications” wording is a supported non-absolute contrast. The key, note and explanation fit the source. The updated castle distractor note is an example-scoped fact about late-medieval fortifications, supported by Historic England. Castle remains a strong but distinguishable distractor. This is an easy, grammatical question with a separate main fact from castle.

**Monastery.** English Heritage describes Cleeve as a monastery housing a community of Cistercian monks and documents the buildings used around its cloister. It also records worship, manual work or manuscript copying, reading sacred texts, and the abbey’s farms and income. This supports the prompt/key and the correct note’s prayer, work, learning and economic-life summary for the cited example. Castle, palace and cathedral name distinct institutions. The question is easy, grammatical, and not a duplicate of cathedral.

**Cathedral.** The Holy See’s general-audience text defines cathedra as the bishop’s established seat in a diocese’s mother church, called a cathedral for that reason. Norwich Cathedral independently states the same relation. The five revised governance fields in the castle, palace, monastery and cathedral cards now explicitly name Norwich Cathedral; its own page directly supports the bounded local claim about the Dean and Chapter, their day-to-day responsibility, and clergy and lay members. The global cathedral prompt remains supported by the Holy See text without limiting the concept to England. Other current notes have source-matched claims. Distractors are distinct medieval institutions/buildings. The question is easy, grammatically aligned, and distinct from the monastery question.

**Knight.** English Heritage directly documents mounted, armoured Norman knights and land granted in return for knight service. Royal Armouries describes knights in the later Hundred Years’ War as a social group expected to go to war, but also notes they were expected to fight on foot. Together these sources show that the current unscoped prompt overgeneralizes mounted service and land tenure across the entire Middle Ages. Narrow it to the Norman/English context; for example, ask about a heavily armoured rider who could receive land for service after the Norman Conquest. The squire note is supported by a Royal Armouries worksheet. The page note remains HOLD: captured primary/institutional sources do not substantiate its court-manners and transition claims, and search snippets are excluded. “Латник” overlaps with an armoured mounted fighter; the note’s vassal/pay claim is not established. Replace it with a distinct role and source-backed note. The current prompt bundles enough historical clues that “easy” may be too low if retained; medium is worth considering.

## Card Games

The four suit questions test four distinct visible suit-to-name relations. The Atlas archive text and inspected historical French-suited specimen support the symbols and colors; Russian suit labels are explicit conventional translations of the archive’s English labels. Bicycle Bridge’s official rules supply each correct note’s separate, game-scoped suit-order fact. Shared wording/options are not automatic duplicates. The answer notes and explanations are supported; permitted note/explanation overlap is not a defect. All four are easy, grammatically sound recognition questions with coherent parallel distractors.

The ace question is independently supported by the original 2015 USPC Bicycle card-face image: the Ace of Spades shows an A index and one central spade pip. Atlas’s inventory page identifies the image as INV 0072. Bicycle’s Spades rules rank A high; its Snip Snap Snorem rules rank A last, showing ace-high and ace-low examples in different games. The rank distractors are plausible adjacent choices, and the King/Queen/Jack ordering is supported in the cited rules. The question is an easy, clear rank-notation fact, distinct from the suit-symbol cards.
