U.S. Copyright Office — What Is Copyright?
Retrieved 2026-10-15; complete official page body read in browser. Relevant sections transcribed from page:

Copyright is a type of intellectual property that protects original works of authorship as soon as an author fixes the work in a tangible form of expression. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, procedures, methods, systems, processes, concepts, principles, or discoveries.

U.S. copyright law provides owners exclusive rights to reproduce a work, prepare derivative works, and distribute copies to the public by sale or other transfer, rental, lease, or lending. Copyright also provides the owner the right to authorize others to exercise these rights, subject to statutory limitations.

A user may use a work by buying or licensing it, seeking permission, relying on statutory exceptions and limitations, or using a public-domain work. Statutory examples include fair use, first sale, and some library/archives reproductions. Public-domain works include works never protected (such as facts or discoveries) or works whose term expired or a previously required formality was not satisfied.

Copyright exists automatically in an original work once fixed; registration is not mandatory, though registration/refusal is needed for U.S. works to enforce exclusive rights in litigation.
