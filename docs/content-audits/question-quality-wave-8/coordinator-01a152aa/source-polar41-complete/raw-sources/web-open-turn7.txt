L406: cite138†Overview cite139†Conservation & Management L407: 
L408: Narwhals are found in the Arctic Ocean. Male narwhals have a tooth that grows into a long clockwise-spiraled tusk, resembling a unicorn horn. Narwhals, like all marine mammals, are protected under the Marine Mammal Protection Act.

L449: ## About the Species
L450: 
L451: cite141†Image: Three narwhals peeking out of the water. Horn of the one in the lead sticks out and forward. Pod of narwhals in northern Canada, August 2005. Credit: Kristin Laidre
L452: 
L453: Pod of narwhals in northern Canada, August 2005. Credit: Kristin Laidre
L454: 
L455: Narwhals are found in the Arctic Ocean. Generally male narwhals have a tooth that grows into a long counter-clockwise-spiraled tusk, resembling the shape of the mythical unicorn horn.
L456: Narwhals, like all marine mammals, are protected under the cite142†Marine Mammal Protection Act .
L457: 
L458: NOAA Fisheries and its partners are working to conserve narwhals and further our understanding of this species through research and conservation activities.
L459: ### Appearance
L460: 
L461: Narwhals have a robust body with a small, bulbous head that has little to no beak and an upturned mouthline. They lack a dorsal fin, and have short, blunt flippers, and flukes with a straight to concave leading edge and convex trailing edge with a deep notch. Older individuals have fluke tips that curl upwards. Adult females can reach lengths up to 13.8 feet and 2,204 pounds and adult males can reach lengths up to 15.7 feet and 3,527.4 pounds.
L462: Young narwhals are uniformly brownish-gray to ray. As juveniles, their skin darkens and develops white mottling, which gives them a spotted appearance. Narwhals continue to develop this white mottling and lighten into adulthood until their skin appears white with black mottling on the head, flippers, dorsal ridge, and flukes.
L463: The tusk of the narwhal is its most unique and distinctive feature, which often leads to comparisons to the resemblance of “unicorns.” Narwhals have two teeth in the upper jaw. These teeth usually remain embedded in the upper jawbone of females. When males reach the age of two or three years, the left tooth normally grows forward, out through the front of the jaw. The tusk is spiraled to the left (counter-clockwise) and may grow up to 9.8 feet long and weigh over 20 pounds.
L464: Occasionally, females with a tusk and males without tusks or with two tusks have been documented. The primary function of the narwhal tusk serves as a secondary sex characteristic of males, for nonviolent assessment of hierarchical status on the basis of relative tusk size and possibly for female mate choice, but it may also be used as a sensory tool for detecting changes in the environment as well as for capturing prey.
L465: ### Behavior and Diet
L466: 
L467: Narwhal pods generally consist of two to ten individuals, but may occur in large dispersed groups of hundreds to thousands of individuals. Smaller groups occur during winter due to the formation of sea ice, and larger groups occur during summer due to open water. Groups may be segregated by age and sex.
L468: Narwhal’s feed at or near the ocean bottom in deep water, and are known to dive down to depths of 3,937 feet for up to 25 minutes. They create suction using their mouth to capture prey. Narwhal’s diet consists of medium to large-size Arctic fish (e.g., Arctic cod, polar cod, and turbot), squid, and shrimp.
L469: ### Where They Live
L470: Narwhals occur in the Arctic Ocean. They generally occur in the Atlantic sector of the Arctic Ocean, with few records in the Pacific sector (i.e., western Russia to Alaska to western Canada). Their main distribution in the Arctic Ocean includes from the central Canada (Peel Sound and northern Hudson Bay) to Greenland to eastern Russia.
L471: Narwhals annually migrate from pack ice in the winter using cracks or holes, to inshore waters in the spring, to coastal waters in the summer following the ice, to open waters in the fall.
L472: ### Lifespan & Reproduction
L473: 
L474: Narwhals live to at least 25 years old, and may live up to 50 years. After a gestation of about 13 to 16 months, narwhals give birth to a single calf in summer (July through August). Calves nurse from their mother for at least a year. Calves are approximately 5 feet in length and 176 pounds at birth.
L475: Species known to undergo menopause include humans, narwhals, cite143†beluga whales , cite144†killer whales , cite145†false killer whales , cite146†short-finned pilot whales , and likely, cite147†bottlenose dolphins . Drawing from our biological understanding of these species, it is plausible that other long-lived marine mammals may also experience menopause.
L476: ### Threats

L480: Narwhals are hunted by native peoples of the Arctic for their flesh, blubber, and tusks.
L481:

L554: ### Conservation Efforts
L555: #### Overseeing Marine Mammal Health and Stranding Response
L556: We work with volunteer networks in all coastal states to respond to marine mammal strandings including all whales. When stranded animals are found alive, NOAA Fisheries and our partners assess the animal’s health and determine the best course of action. When stranded animals are found dead, our scientists work to understand and investigate the cause of death.
L557: Although the cause often remains unknown, scientists can sometimes attribute strandings to disease, harmful algal blooms, vessel strikes, fishing gear entanglements, pollution exposure, and underwater noise. Some strandings can serve as indicators of ocean health, giving insight into larger environmental issues that may also have implications for human health and welfare.
L558: cite158†Learn more about the Marine Mammal Health and Stranding Response Program L559: #### Marine Mammal Unusual Mortality Events
L560: 
L561: Narwhals have never been part of a declared unusual mortality event. Under the cite142†Marine Mammal Protection Act , an unusual mortality event (UME) is defined as "a stranding that is unexpected; involves a significant die-off of any marine mammal population; and demands immediate response." To understand the health of marine mammal populations, scientists study unusual mortality events.
