L468: 
L469: Pod of killer whales. Credit: NOAA Fisheries
L470: 
L471: The killer whale, also known as orca, is the ocean’s top predator. It is the largest member of the Delphinidae family, or dolphins. Members of this family include all dolphin species, as well as other larger species, such as cite146†long-finned pilot whales and cite147†short-finned pilot whales , whose common names also contain "whale" instead of "dolphin."
L472: Found in every ocean in the world, they are the most widely distributed of all cetaceans (whales and dolphins). Scientific studies have revealed many cite148†different populations with several distinct ecotypes (or forms) of killer whales worldwide—some of which may be different species or subspecies. They are one of the most recognizable marine mammals, with their distinctive black and white bodies. Globally, killer whales occur in a wide range of habitats, in both open seas and coastal waters.
L473: Taken as a whole, the species has the most varied diet of all cetaceans, but different populations are usually specialized in their foraging behavior and diet. They often use a coordinated hunting strategy, working as a team like a pack of wolves.
L474: Hunters and fishermen once targeted killer whales. As a result, historical threats to killer whales included commercial hunting and culling to protect fisheries from killer whales. In addition, although live capture of killer whales for aquarium display and marine parks no longer occurs in the United States, it continues to remain a threat globally.
L475: Today, some killer whale populations face many other threats, including food limitations, chemical contaminants, and disturbances from vessel traffic and sound. Efforts to establish critical habitat, set protective regulations, and restore prey stocks are essential to conservation, especially for the endangered Southern Resident killer whale population.
L476: All killer whale populations are protected under the cite149†Marine Mammal Protection Act . Only two populations receive additional special protections under federal law:
L477: 
L478:   * Southern Resident Distinct Population Segment (DPS) (listed as endangered under the cite150†Endangered Species Act and designated as depleted under the MMPA )
L479:   * AT1 Transient stock (designated as cite151†depleted under the MMPA)
L480: cite139†Southern Resident killer whales are the only endangered population of killer whales in the United States, ranging from central California to southeast Alaska. Long-term commitments across state and international borders are needed to stabilize the Southern Resident population and prevent their extinction. The Southern Resident killer whale is one of NOAA Fisheries' cite152†Species in the Spotlight .
L481: This initiative includes animals considered most at risk for extinction and prioritizes recovery efforts.
L482: NOAA Fisheries is committed to the conservation of killer whales and the recovery of endangered populations. Our scientists and partners use a variety of innovative techniques to study and protect them. We also work with our partners to develop regulations and management plans that protect killer whales and their food sources, decrease contaminants in oceans, reduce ocean noise, and raise awareness about the whales and the actions people can take to support their recovery.
L483: ### Population Status
L484: 
L485: Several different populations and ecotypes of killer whales are found throughout the world. NOAA Fisheries estimates population size in our cite153†stock assessment reports . It is estimated that there are around 50,000 killer whales globally. Approximately 2,500 killer whales live in the eastern North Pacific Ocean—home to the most well-studied killer whale populations.
L486: In recent decades, several populations of killer whales have declined and some have become endangered. The population of AT1 Transients, a stock of transient killer whales in the eastern North Pacific, has been reduced from 22 to 6 whales since the 1989 Exxon Valdez oil spill. In 2004, NOAA Fisheries designated this stock as depleted under the MMPA based on the cite154†results of the status review†repository.library.noaa.gov (PDF, 25 pages).
L487: Scientists estimate the minimum historical population size of cite139†Southern Residents in the eastern North Pacific was about 140 animals. Following live-capture in the 1960s for use in marine mammal parks, 71 animals remained in 1974. Although there was some growth in the population in the 1970s and 1980s, with a peak of 98 animals in 1995, the population experienced a decline of almost 20 percent in the late 1990s, leaving 80 whales in 2001.

L177: Overview
L178: 
L179: The polar bear (Ursus maritimus) is the largest of the extant bear species and is classified as a marine mammal. Polar bears are protected under the cite62†Endangered Species Act and cite63†Marine Mammal Protection Act . The Marine Mammal Protection Act prohibits the take, which is defined as harass, hunt, capture, or kill, of all marine mammals, with an exception in place for harvest by Alaska Native peoples.
L180: The global polar bear population is estimated to be 26,000 individuals as of 2023. Largely dependent on sea ice, their native range lies throughout the circumpolar Arctic, primarily above the Arctic Circle, which is one of the largest ranges for an extant large carnivore. Polar bears occur in 19 subpopulations throughout the seasonally and permanently ice-covered marine waters of Arctic and Subarctic regions of Canada, Greenland, Norway, Russia and the United States.
L181: Population status varies for each subpopulation. The U.S. contains portions of two subpopulations: the Chukchi Sea and the Southern Beaufort Sea.
L182: Genetic research has confirmed that polar bears evolved from brown bears (U. arctos) roughly 500 thousand years ago. Polar bears and brown bears overlap in regions of northern Canada and Alaska, as well as eastern Russia.
L183: Polar bears are top predators in the Arctic marine ecosystem. Polar bears prey heavily on ringed seals (Phoca hispida) and, to a lesser extent, bearded seals (Erignathus barbatus). They occasionally take larger animals such as walruses (Odobenus rosmarus) and belugas (Delphinapterus leucas).
L184: The remains of bowhead whales (Balaena mysticetus) from subsistence harvest, strandings or orca (Orcinus orca) predation have been an important and seasonally reliable food source in some regions, including the Southern Beaufort Sea, Svalbard and Chukotka, Russia. In general, polar bears have a similar dependence upon sea ice habitats, rely upon similar prey and exhibit similar life history characteristics throughout their range.
L185: Polar bears are a K-selected or slow species, characterized by late sexual maturity, small litter sizes and extended parental investment in raising young. All these factors contribute to the low reproductive rate of the species. Reproduction in the female polar bear is similar to that in other Ursids, or bears. Females generally mature and breed for the first time at 4 to 5 years and give birth at 5 to 6 years of age.
L186: Litter size typically ranges between one to three cubs, with litters of two cubs being most common. Cubs typically remain with their mother until they are 2 years old. Consequently, the minimum reproductive interval for adult females with a successful litter is 3 years. Most males reach sexual maturity around 6 years of age, although some may breed as young as 3 or 4 years old.
L187: Denning is a critical period in polar bear life history. Females enter dens between September and December. A successful denning period requires adequate thermal protection, time for cub maturation, a lack of natural or human disturbance and security from predation. Most black (U. americanus) and brown bears of all sex and age classes spend several winter months inside a den in a state of metabolic dormancy to conserve energy. However, only pregnant female polar bears overwinter in dens.

L4@P0: STOCK DEFINITION AND GEOGRAPHIC RANGE
L5@P0: The family Odobenidae is represented by a single modern species, Odobenus rosmarus of
L6@P0: which two sub-species are generally recognized: the Atlantic walrus (O. r. rosmarus), which
L7@P0: ranges from the central Canadian Arctic eastward to the Kara Sea (Reeves 1978), and the Pacific
L8@P0: walrus (O. r. divergens) which ranges across the Bering and Chukchi Seas (Fay 1982, Lowry
L9@P0: 2016). The Pacific walrus is the only sub-species occurring in United States (U.S.) waters and
L10@P0: considered in this account. Genetic studies suggest that Pacific walruses are represented by a
L11@P0: single panmictic population (Scribner et al. 1997, Sonsthagen et al. 2012, Beatty et al. 2020).
L12@P0: Pacific walruses inhabit the shallow continental shelf waters of the Bering and Chukchi
L13@P0: seas, occasionally moving into the East Siberian Sea and the Beaufort Sea (Fig. 1). Walruses
L14@P0: forage primarily on benthic invertebrates and rest between feeding bouts either on sea ice or land.
L15@P0: Figure 1. Seasonal distribution, breeding areas, and coastal haulouts of Pacific walruses in
L16@P0-1: the Bering and Chukchi seas.2
L17@P1: During the winter breeding season, walruses are found in areas of the Bering Sea where open
L18@P1: leads, polynyas, or thin ice occur (Fay et al. 1984; Fig. 1). Although many adult male Pacific
L19@P1: walruses remain in the Bering Sea during the ice-free season where they forage from coastal
L20@P1: haulouts, most of the population (primarily females and juveniles) migrates north in summer and
L21@P1: south in winter following seasonal patterns of sea ice advance and retreat (Fay 1982).

L50@P1: The Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA) defines a minimum population estimate
L51@P1: (NMIN) as an estimate of the number of animals in a stock that: 1) Is based on the best available
L52@P1: scientific information on abundance, incorporating the precision and variability associated with
L53@P1: such information; and 2) Provides reasonable assurance that the stock size is equal to or greater
L54@P1: than the estimate (MMPA §3(27)). The NMIN for a marine mammal stock is often calculated
L55@P1: using Equation 1 from NMFS’s potential biological removal guidelines: NMIN =
L56@P1: N/exp(0.842×[ln(1+[CV(N)2])]½), which approximates the 20th percentile of a log-normal

L60@P2: NMIN (Beatty et al. 2022; Fig. 2).

L64@P2: Current Population Trend
L65@P2: Based on large, sustained harvests in the 18th and 19th centuries, Fay (1982) speculated
L66@P2: that the pre-exploitation population was represented by a minimum of 200,000 animals. Largescale commercial harvests are thought to have reduced the population to less than 100,000
L67@P2: animals by the mid-1950s (Fay et al. 1997). The population appears to have rapidly increased in
L68@P2: size during the 1960s and 1970s in response to harvest regulations that limited the harvesting of
L69@P2: females (Fay et al. 1989, Fay et al. 1997, Garlich-Miller et al. 2006). By the 1980s, walrus
L70@P2: researchers were concerned that the population had exceeded its natural carrying capacity and
L71@P2: warned that a combination of density-dependent mechanisms and rising harvest rates in both the
L72@P2: U.S. and Russia (Fig. 3) could lead to a population decline (Fay et al. 1989, Fay et al. 1997).
L73@P2: Taylor et al. (2018) used Bayesian models to assess walrus population dynamics using available
L74@P2: information regarding historic estimates of abundance, population demographics, and harvest
L75@P2: removals. Selected models indicated that the Pacific walrus population underwent a sharp
L76@P2: decline in the 1980s which began to moderate in the 1990s as harvest rates declined and
L77@P2: reproductive and calf survival rates began to increase in a density‐dependent manner. Selected
L78@P2: models were, however, equivocal regarding whether population growth rates were positive or
L79@P2: negative for the period 1999–2015 (Taylor et al. 2018).
L80@P2: Although the most recent (2017) point estimate of abundance (257,192) is substantially
L81@P2: larger than the 2006 aerial survey-based estimate (129,000), the two estimates are statistically
L82@P2: indistinguishable due to overlapping credibility intervals (Table 1) and known negative biases
L83@P2: associated with the 2006 aerial survey effort (Table 2, Beatty et al. 2022, Speckman et al. 2011).
L84@P2: Thus, the current population trend for this stock is unknown. An integrated population model
L85@P2: that incorporates recently available data sets regarding abundance, age-structure, and harvest
L86@P2: removals could help to improve our understanding of past, present, and likely future population
L87@P2-3: trends (Beatty et al. 2022).4
L88@P3: CURRENT AND MAXIMUM NET PRODUCTIVITY RATES
L89@P3: Published estimates of net productivity rates for walrus populations range from 3 percent
L90@P3: to 13 percent per year (Chapskii 1936, DeMaster 1984, Sease and Chapman 1988, Fay et al.
L91@P3: 1997). Chivers (1999) developed an individual age-based model of the Pacific walrus population
L92@P3: using published estimates of survival and reproduction which yielded a theoretical maximum
L93@P3: population growth rate (RMAX) of 8 percent but cautioned that age-specific survival rates for
L94@P3: Pacific walruses were poorly known. In the absence of stock-specific derived values, a default
L95@P3: RMAX value of 12 percent is recommended for pinniped stocks (NMFS 2016). Whereas most
L96@P3: pinnipeds can produce an offspring annually, walruses have a lower reproductive rate due to
L97@P3: their biennial reproductive cycle (Fay 1982, Garlich-Miller et al. 2006). An RMAX value of 6
L98@P3: percent (one half the default pinniped value) has been adopted for the Pacific walrus stock
L99@P3: pending the publication of stock-specific values. The Service is in the process of developing a
L100@P3: walrus population model incorporating the best available estimates of population size, agestructure, growth rate, carrying capacity and harvest demographics from which stock specific
L101@P3: estimates of RMAX can be derived.
L102@P3: POTENTIAL BIOLOGICAL REMOVAL
L103@P3: The potential biological removal (PBR) of a marine mammal stock is defined under the
L104@P3: MMPA as the maximum number of animals, not including natural mortalities, that may be
L105@P3: removed from a marine mammal stock while allowing that stock to reach or maintain its
L106@P3: optimum sustainable population (OSP). The PBR is the product of the following factors: (A) the
L107@P3: minimum population estimate NMIN, (B) one-half the maximum net productivity rate (0.5 RMAX),
L108@P3: and (C) a recovery factor (FR) ranging between 0.1 and 1.0 (MMPA §3(20)).
