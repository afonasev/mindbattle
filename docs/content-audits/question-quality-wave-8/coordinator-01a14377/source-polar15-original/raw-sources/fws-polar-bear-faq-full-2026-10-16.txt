L1: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Proposal to List Polar Bears as Threatened Species
L2: Where do polar bears live?
L3: Polar bears evolved to utilize the Arctic sea ice niche and are distributed throughout most ice-covered
L4: seas of the Northern Hemisphere, which include areas of the United States, Canada, Greenland,
L5: Norway, and Russia. They are generally limited to areas where the sea is ice-covered for much of the
L6: year. They are not evenly distributed throughout this Arctic habitat, nor do they comprise a single
L7: nomadic population, but rather occur in 19 relatively discrete populations. Scientists have described
L8: the boundaries of these populations based on behavioral and ecological factors and after decades of
L9: intensive scientific studies and information from Native communities. These populations often cross
L10: international boundaries; the United States, for example, shares polar populations with both Russia and
L11: Canada.
L12: Polar bears are most abundant near the shore in shallow-water areas, and in other areas where currents
L13: and ocean upwelling increase marine productivity and serve to keep the ice cover from becoming too
L14: solidified in winter. Over most of their range, polar bears remain on the sea ice year-round or spend at
L15: most only short periods on land. They occur throughout the East Siberian, Laptev, and Kara Seas of
L16: Russia, Fram Strait and Greenland Sea, Barents Sea of northern Europe, Baffin Bay between Canada
L17: and Greenland, through most of the Canadian Arctic archipelago, and in the Chukchi and Beaufort
L18: Seas located to the west and north of Alaska.
L19: What is the current status of world polar bear populations?
L20: The total number of polar bears worldwide is estimated to be 20,000-25,000. Within Alaska, there are
L21: an estimated 4,700 animals shared with Canada and Russia in three populations – 1,500 in the
L22: Southern Beaufort Sea, 1,200 in the Northern Beaufort Sea, and 2,000 in the Chukchi Sea. The former
L23: two populations are shared with Canada, and the latter with Russia. There are no overall data on
L24: global polar bear population trends. However, long-term scientific studies in Canada’s Western
L25: Hudson Bay have identified reduced adult weights and cub survivorship, resulting in a decline of that
L26: population to an estimated 935 animals -- a 22% decline -- correlated with loss of sea ice. More recent
L27: studies of the estimated 1,200 individuals in the Southern Beaufort Sea population in Alaska do not
L28: currently show a statistically significant decline, but this population is now experiencing the same
L29: pattern of reduced adult weights and cub survival as Western Hudson Bay. While such detailed
L30: studies are not available for other polar bear populations, the Service believes they may be facing the
L31: same situation, given their similar life history.
L32: The projected threat to polar bears is the worldwide loss of their sea ice habitat. Recent data indicate a
L33: rapid and unprecedented retreat of Arctic sea ice (including earlier spring melt, later fall freeze-up and
L34: overall thinner ice) and there are projections of an ice-free Arctic Ocean within the foreseeable future.
L35: Since polar bears live on sea ice for a majority of the year and depend upon sea ice habitats for their
L36: key life functions, loss of sea ice would detrimentally affect all polar bears, world-wide.
L37@P0-1: This primary potential threat to polar bears appears to be unique because it stems from conditions throughout the Arctic rather than in any one localized area. The future status of the species is being
L38: predicted through models of the projected effects of sea ice change on polar bear populations which
L39: still need further development, testing, and enhancement before a final decision can be made about
L40: their future status.

L42: What is the process for addressing a petition to add a species to the list of threatened and
L43: endangered species under the Endangered Species Act?
L44: The Endangered Species Act requires that the Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) make a finding on
L45: whether a petition to list, delist, or reclassify a species presents substantial information indicating that
L46: the petitioned action may be warranted. This finding is based on information contained in the petition,
L47: supporting information submitted with the petition, and information otherwise available to the FWS at
L48: the time of the finding. To the maximum extent practicable, the Service makes this finding within 90-
L49: days of the receipt of the petition and publishes this 90-day finding promptly in the Federal Register.
L50: If the Service finds that substantial information is presented, it commences a review of the status of the
L51: species which is to be completed, if feasible, within 12 months of receipt of the petition. In the 12-
L52: month finding, one of three determinations can be made: (1) the petitioned action is not warranted; (2)
L53: the petitioned action is warranted, but precluded by other pending listing actions; or (3) the petitioned
L54: action is warranted, and the species is proposed for listing.

L58: on currently available data, that there is sufficient scientific evidence of a global threat to the polar

L60: lengthy process which was initiated in 2005 and which will still require much additional work to
L61: enhance existing scientific models and analyses before a final decision can be made on whether to list
L62: the species.
L63: The Service now will actively seek additional scientific and commercial data, information, and
L64: comments on the proposed rule. The Service will accept comments for 90 days following the
L65: publication of the 12-month finding in the Federal Register, and will also hold one or more public
L66: hearings where the public can obtain information and offer comments. A copy of the proposed rule
L67: and other information about the proposal is available on the Internet at http://alaska.fws.gov. A final
L68: decision will take into account these comments and any other new information the Service obtains,

L70: What are the criteria for listing a species as threatened or endangered under the ESA?
L71: The Service’s current proposal is to list the polar bear as “Threatened” throughout its range. As we
L72: evaluate public comments and any new information, we will consider all possible actions. By
L73: definition in the ESA, an “Endangered” species is likely to go extinct within all or a significant portion
L74: of its range, while a “Threatened” species is likely to become Endangered in the foreseeable future.
L75: Under threatened status the species is protected and managed for recovery, but FWS may also adopt
L76@P1-2: special rules tailored to the conservation needs of the species. The ESA requires that a species be listed if it is imperiled by one or more of the following five criteria:
L77: • Present or threatened destruction, modification or curtailment of its habitat or range;
L78: • Overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific or educational purposes;
L79: • Disease or predation;
L80: • Inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms; or
L81: • Other natural or manmade factors affecting its continued existence.
L82: Thus identification of any one of these factors as a threat to a species can require the listing of the
L83: species under the ESA. In the case of the polar bear, the melting of sea ice is the potential threat to the
L84: species. The ESA does not discriminate between natural or manmade causes.
L85: Which of these ESA criteria is judged to be most important to the future status of the polar
L86: bear?
L87: In the case of polar bears, the decision to propose this listing as Threatened is based on the future
L88: effect of the continued expected modification or curtailment of its habitat or range, specifically from
L89: receding sea ice, and the absence of any known regulatory mechanisms at the national or international
L90: level effectively addressing this threat to polar bear habitat.
L91: The ESA uses the term: “foreseeable future”; what is this?
L92: The ESA does not define “foreseeable future.” In other ESA listings, it has often been interpreted to be
L93: a function of generations of the species in question and/or habitat regeneration cycles. In this status
L94: review, based on the opinion of polar bear experts, the Service has adopted three generations as the
L95: upper limit. Using this measure, since a polar bear generation is defined as 15 years, the “foreseeable
L96: future” addresses the next 45 years.
L97: If the final decision is made to list the polar bear under the ESA, how would this listing help the
L98: species?
L99: The ESA requires that decisions be made solely on the basis of the five listing criteria outlined above,
L100: without regard to the level of knowledge or ability to address the threats to the species. A final
L101: decision to list the polar bear as threatened would not have any direct effect on the predicted reduction
L102: in sea ice habitat. However, listing would require the initiation of a recovery planning process, unless
L103: it is determined that this would not promote the conservation of the species. This planning process
L104: would include the cooperative efforts of International, Federal, State and local officials and agencies,
L105: Arctic Native groups, industry, and private entities to identify practical and feasible measures to
L106: provide for conservation of the species. These efforts would help increase public awareness about the
L107: status of polar bears and would assist in developing and implementing future polar bear management
L108: strategies. Listing would also require Federal agencies to consult (under Section 7 of the ESA) with
L109: the Service for any actions which might affect polar bears within the United States.
L110: Is sport hunting or subsistence harvesting of polar bears legal today?
L111@P2-3: Hunting polar bears is prohibited by Norway and Russia, although some illegal harvest is occurring in Russia. Canada and Greenland allow subsistence take by Native communities as well as regulated
L112: sport hunting of certain populations. In the United States, the Marine Mammal Protection Act
L113: prohibits sport hunting, but subsistence harvest of polar bears by Alaskan Natives is allowed. A user
L114: group management agreement is in place between the Inupiat of Alaska and the Inuvialuit of Canada
L115: to help ensure that subsistence harvest of the shared Beaufort Sea polar bear population is sustainable.
L116: Legislation to implement a bilateral agreement between the United States and Russia, which would
L117: provide for joint management and regulation of harvest of shared populations in the Chukchi Sea, has
L118: passed Congress and is now awaiting presentation to the President for signature.

L146: Are there reliable figures for changes in the Arctic sea ice cap over the last several decades?
L147: Observations have shown a decline in late summer Arctic sea ice to the extent of 7.7 percent per
L148@P3-4: decade and in the perennial sea ice area of 9.8 percent per decade since 1978. Observations have likewise shown a thinning of the Arctic sea ice of 32 percent from the 1960s and 1970s to the 1990s in

L150: Why is the sea ice melting?
L151: The predominant reasons for amplified decreases in the extent of sea ice are: (a) the sea ice albedo
L152: effect (i.e., less sea ice cover, which has a high reflectivity, causes more absorption of solar radiation
L153: in the ocean and hence more heat storage in the ocean, and a warmer ocean further delays formation of
L154: new sea ice cover in the fall); (b) the thinning of the sea ice (including the reduction in perennial ice),
L155: which leads to more rapid melting of sea ice; (c) an increase in melt season length, which enhances the
L156: ice albedo feedback, and decrease in ice season length, which limits the winter ice extent and the
L157: average thickness of ice during the season; and (d) the movement of ice out of the Arctic Ocean. The
L158: National Snow and Ice Data Center in Boulder, Colorado, using satellite images, estimates that the
L159: Arctic ice cap has shrunk by 20% since 1979.
L160: Why couldn’t polar bears adapt to these changes in their habitat?
L161: Genetic research indicates the polar bears evolved from an isolated population of the grizzly or brown
L162: bears 250,000 years ago. They are usually considered marine mammals since they are highly adapted
L163: to life on sea ice. Their fur, short snout, and small ears are adaptations to the cold; their teeth are
L164: specialized for a completely carnivorous diet (primarily of arctic seals); their feet have tiny papilae and
L165: “suction cups” for increased traction on ice; and their claws are shorter and more curved than grizzly
L166: bears. Their body structure and locomotion is adapted to walking on ice and swimming between ice
L167: flows, and they are not as efficient in walking or running on land as grizzly bears. If polar bears had to
L168: adapt to spending more of their lives on land, they would have to compete with grizzlies and other
L169: predators for prey items for which they are not as well adapted. Some polar bears spend portions of
L170: their on land waiting for the ice to return – for example, the Western Hudson Bay population – but
L171: during these periods when they do not have access to their regular food supply, they typically do not
L172: eat and instead live off their stored fat reserves. All of these factors would mean that it could be
L173: difficult for polar bears to adapt to living without ice.

L199: Specifically, how does loss of sea ice threaten polar bears?
L200: Although some females will use snow dens on land for birthing cubs, polar bears are almost
L201: completely dependent upon Arctic sea-ice for survival. They use sea ice as a platform from which to
L202: hunt and feed upon seals, to seek mates and breed, to move to maternity denning areas on land, and to
L203: travel long distances. Thus any significant changes in the abundance, distribution, or existence of sea
L204: ice would have profound effects all stages of the animal’s life cycle.
