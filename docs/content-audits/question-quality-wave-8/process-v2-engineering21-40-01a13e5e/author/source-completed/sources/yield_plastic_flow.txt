L6@P0: Introduction
L7@P0: In our overview of the tensile stress-strain curve in Module 4, we described yield as a permanent
L8@P0: molecular rearrangement that begins at a sufficiently high stress, denoted σY in Fig. 1. The
L9@P0: yielding process is very material-dependent, being related directly to molecular mobility. It is
L10@P0: often possible to control the yielding process by optimizing the materials processing in a way
L11@P0: that influences mobility. General purpose polystyrene, for instance, is a weak and brittle plastic
L12@P0: often credited with giving plastics a reputation for shoddiness that plagued the industry for
L13@P0: years. This occurs because polystyrene at room temperature has so little molecular mobility
L14@P0: that it experiences brittle fracture at stresses less than those needed to induce yield with its
L15@P0: associated ductile flow. But when that same material is blended with rubber particles of suitable
L16@P0: size and composition, it becomes so tough that it is used for batting helmets and ultra-durable
L17@P0: children’s toys. This magic is done by control of the yielding process. Yield control to balance
L18@P0: strength against toughness is one of the most important aspects of materials engineering for
L19@P0: structural applications, and all engineers should be aware of the possibilities.
L20@P0: Figure 1: Yield stress σY as determined by the 0.2% offset method.
L21@P0: Another important reason for understanding yield is more prosaic: if the material is not
L22@P0: allowed to yield, it is not likely to fail. This is not true of brittle materials such as ceramics that
L23@P0: fracture before they yield, but in most of the tougher structural materials no damage occurs
L24@P0: before yield. It is common design practice to size the structure so as to keep the stresses in the
L25@P0: elastic range, short of yield by a suitable safety factor. We therefore need to be able to predict
L26@P0-1: 1when yielding will occur in general multidimensional stress states, given an experimental value
L27@P1: of σY .
L28@P1: Fracture is driven by normal stresses, acting to separate one atomic plane from another.
L29@P1: Yield, conversely, is driven by shearing stresses, sliding one plane along another. These two
L30@P1: distinct mechanisms are illustrated n Fig. 2. Of course, bonds must be broken during the sliding
L31@P1: associated with yield, but unlike in fracture are allowed to reform in new positions. This process
L32@P1: can generate substantial change in the material, even leading eventually to fracture (as in bending
L33@P1: a metal rod back and forth repeatedly to break it). The “plastic” deformation that underlies
L34@P1: yielding is essentially a viscous flow process, and follows kinetic laws quite similar to liquids.
L35@P1: Like flow in liquids, plastic flow usually takes place without change in volume, corresponding to
L36@P1: Poisson’s ratio ν =1/2.
L37@P1: Figure 2: Cracking is caused by normal stresses (a), sliding is caused by shear stresses (b).
L38@P1: Multiaxial stress states
L39@P1: The yield stress σY is usually determined in a tensile test, where a single uniaxial stress acts.
L40@P1: However, the engineer must be able to predict when yield will occur in more complicated real-life
L41@P1: situations involving multiaxial stresses. This is done by use of a yield criterion, an observation
L42@P1: derived from experimental evidence as to just what it is about the stress state that causes yield.
L43@P1: One of the simplest of these criteria, known as the maximum shear stress or Tresca criterion,
L44@P1: states that yield occurs when the maximum shear stress reaches a critical value τmax = k. The
L45@P1: numerical value of k for a given material could be determined directly in a pure-shear test, such
L46@P1: as torsion of a circular shaft, but it can also be found indirectly from the tension test as well. As
L47@P1: shown in Fig. 3, Mohr’s circle shows that the maximum shear stress acts on a plane 45◦ away
L48@P1: from the tensile axis, and is half the tensile stress in magnitude; then k = σY /2.
L49@P1: In cases of plane stress, Mohr’s circle gives the maximum shear stress in that plane as half
L50@P1: the difference of the principal stresses:

L152@P5: Effect of hydrostatic pressure
L153@P5: Since in the discussion up to now yield has been governed only by shear stress, it has not
L154@P5: mattered whether a uniaxial stress is compressive or tensile; yield occurs when σ = ±σY . This
L155@P5: corresponds to the hydrostatic component of the stress −p =(σx+σy+σz)/3 having no influence
L156@P5: on yield, which is observed experimentally to be valid for slip in metallic systems. Polymers,
L157@P5: however, are much more resistant to yielding in compressive stress states than in tension. The
L158@P5: atomistic motions underlying slip in polymers can be viewed as requiring “free volume” as the
L159@P5: molecular segments move, and this free volume is diminished by compressive stresses. It is thus
L160@P5: difficult to form solid polymers by deformation processing such as stamping and forging in the
L161@P5: same way steel can be shaped; this is one reason the vast majority of automobile body panels
L162@P5: continue to be made of steel rather than plastic.
L163@P5: Figure 8: Effect of pressure on the von Mises yield envelope.

L165@P6: state that yield occurs when

L168@P6: more positive) the shear stress needed for yield becomes greater as well, since there is less free
L169@P6: volume and more hindrance to molecular motion. The effect of this modification is to slide the
L170@P6: von Mises ellipse to extend less into the I quadrant and more into the III quadrant as shown
L171@P6: in Fig. 8. This shows graphically that greater stresses are needed for yield in compression, and
L172@P6: lesser stresses in tension.

L175@P6: Several amorphous glassy polymers — notably polystyrene, polymethylmethacrylate, and
L176@P6: polycarbonate — are subject to a yield mechanism termed “crazing” in which long elongated
L177@P6: voids are created within the material by a tensile cavitation process. Figure 9 shows a craze
L178@P6: in polystyrene, grown in plasticizing fluid near Tg. The voids, or crazes, are approximately
L179@P6: 1000˚A thick and microns or more in length, and appear visually to be much like conventional
L180@P6: cracks. They differ from cracks, however, in that the broad faces of the crazes are spanned by
L181@P6: a great many elongated fibrils that have been drawn from the polymer as the craze opens. The
L182@P6: fibril formation requires shear flow, but the process is also very dependent on free volume. A
L183@P6: successful multiaxial stress criterion for crazing that incorporates both these features has been
L184@P6: proposed3 of the form

L189@P6: the hydrostatic tension increases, the shear needed to cause crazing decreases. The parameters
L190@P6: A and B are adjustable, and both depend on temperature. This relation plots as a batwing on

L196@P7: Crazing is a yield mechanism, but it also precipitates brittle fracture as the craze height
L197@P7: increases and the fibrils are brought to rupture. The point where the craze locus crosses the
L198@P7: shear yielding locus is therefore a type of mechanically induced ductile-brittle transition, as the
L199@P7: failure mode switches from shear yielding to craze embrittlement. Environmental agents such
L200@P7: as acetone that expand the free volume in these polymers greatly exacerbate the tendency for
L201@P7: craze brittleness. Conversely, modifications such as rubber particle inclusions that stabilize the
L202@P7: crazes and prevent them from becoming true cracks can provide remarkable toughness. Rubber
L203@P7: particles not only stabilize crazes, they also cause a great increase in the number of crazes, so
L204@P7: the energy absorption of craze formation can add to the toughness as well. This is the basis of
L205@P7: the “high impact polystyrene,” or HIPS, mentioned at the outset of this chapter.
L206@P7: Effect of rate and temperature
L207@P7: The yield process can be viewed as competing with fracture, and whichever process has the
L208@P7: lowest stress requirements will dominate. As the material is made less and less mobile, for
L209@P7: instance by lowering the temperature or increasing the number and tightness of chemical bonds,
L210@P7: yielding becomes more and more difficult. The fracture process is usually much less dependent
L211@P7: on mobility. Both yield and fracture stresses usually increase with decreasing temperature, but
L212@P7: yield is more temperature-dependent (see Fig. 11). This implies that below a critical temperature
L213@P7: (called the ductile-brittle transition temperature TDB) the material will fracture before it yields.
L214@P7: Several notable failures in ships and pipelines have occurred during winter temperatures when
L215@P7: the steels used in their manufacture were stressed below their TDB and were thus unable to
L216@P7: resist catastrophic crack growth. In polymers, the ductile-brittle transition temperature is often
L217@P7: coincident with the glass transition temperature. Clearly, we need an engineering model capable
L218@P7: of showing how yield depends on temperature, and one popular approach is outlined below.

L271@P9: Continuum plasticity
L272@P9: Plasticity theory, which seeks to determine stresses and displacements in structures all or part
L273@P9: of which have been stressed beyond the yield point, is an important aspect of solid mechanics.
L274@P9: The situation is both materially and geometrically nonlinear, so it is not a trivial undertaking.
L275@P9: However, in such areas as metal forming, plasticity theory has provided valuable insight. We
L276@P9: will outline only a few aspects of this field in the following paragraphs, to introduce some of the
L277@P9: fundamental concepts that the reader can extend in future study.
L278@P9: Plastic deformation
L279@P9: A useful idealization in modeling plastic behavior takes the material to be linearly elastic up
L280@P9: to the yield point as shown in Fig. 13, and then “perfectly plastic” at strains beyond yield.
L281@P9: Strains up to yield (the line between points a and b ) are recoverable, and the material unloads
L282@P9: along the same elastic line it followed during loading; this is conventional elastic response. But
L283@P9: if the material is strained beyond yield (point b), the “plastic” straining beyond b takes place at
L284@P9: constant stress and is unrecoverable. If the material is strained to point c and then unloaded, it
L285@P9: follows the path cd (a line parallel to the original elastic line ab) rather than returning along cba.
L286@P9: When the stress has been brought to zero (point d), the plastic strain ad remains as a residual
L287@P9: strain.
L288@P9: Plastic deformation can generate “ residual” stresses in structures, internal stresses that
L289@P9: remain even after the external loads are removed. To illustrate this, consider two rods having
L290@P9: different stress-strain curves, connected in parallel (so their strains are always equal) as shown
L291@P9: in Fig. 14. When the rods are strained up to the yield point of rod B (point a on the strain
L292@P9-10: 10Figure 13: The elastic-perfectly plastic idealization of plastic deformation.
L293@P10: axis), rod A will have experienced an amount of permanent plastic deformation p. When the
L294@P10: applied load is removed, rod B unloads along its original stress-strain curve, but rod A follows
L295@P10: a path parallel to its original elastic line. When rod A reaches zero stress (point b), rod B will
L296@P10: still be in tension (point c). In order for the load transmitted by the rods together to come to
L297@P10: zero, rod B will pull rod A into compression until −σB = σA as indicated by points d and e.
L298@P10: Residual stresses are left in the rods, and the assembly as a whole is left with a residual tensile
L299@P10: strain.
L300@P10: Figure 14: Plastic deformation of two-bar assembly.
L301@P10: Compressive residual stress can be valuable if the structure must bear tensile loads. Similarly
L302@P10: to how rapid quenching can be used to make safety glass by putting the surfaces in compression,
L303@P10: plastic deformation can be used to create favorable compressive stresses. One famous such
L304@P10: technique is called “autofrettage;” this is a method used to strengthen cannon barrels against
L305@P10: bursting by pressurizing them from the inside so as to bring the inner portion of the barrel into
L306@P10: the plastic range. When the pressure is removed, the inner portions are left with a compressive
L307@P10: residual stress just as with bar A in the above example.
