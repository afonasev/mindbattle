L6@P0: Introduction
L7@P0: Stress-strain curves are an extremely important graphical measure of a material’s mechanical
L8@P0: properties, and all students of Mechanics of Materials will encounter them often. However, they
L9@P0: are not without some subtlety, especially in the case of ductile materials that can undergo substantial geometrical change during testing. This module will provide an introductory discussion
L10@P0: of several points needed to interpret these curves, and in doing so will also provide a preliminary
L11@P0: overview of several aspects of a material’s mechanical properties. However, this module will
L12@P0: not attempt to survey the broad range of stress-strain curves exhibited by modern engineering
L13@P0: materials (the atlas by Boyer cited in the References section can be consulted for this). Several
L14@P0: of the topics mentioned here — especially yield and fracture — will appear with more detail in
L15@P0: later modules.
L16@P0: “Engineering” Stress-Strain Curves
L17@P0: Perhaps the most important test of a material’s mechanical response is the tensile test1, in which
L18@P0: one end of a rod or wire specimen is clamped in a loading frame and the other subjected to
L19@P0: a controlled displacement δ (see Fig. 1). A transducer connected in series with the specimen
L20@P0: provides an electronic reading of the load P(δ) corresponding to the displacement. Alternatively,
L21@P0: modern servo-controlled testing machines permit using load rather than displacement as the
L22@P0: controlled variable, in which case the displacement δ(P) would be monitored as a function of
L23@P0: load.

L36@P1: In the early (low strain) portion of the curve, many materials obey Hooke’s law to a reasonable approximation, so that stress is proportional to strain with the constant of proportionality
L37@P1: being the modulus of elasticity or Young’s modulus, denoted E:
L38@P1: σe = Ee (2)
L39@P1: As strain is increased, many materials eventually deviate from this linear proportionality,
L40@P1: the point of departure being termed the proportional limit. This nonlinearity is usually associated with stress-induced “plastic” flow in the specimen. Here the material is undergoing
L41@P1: a rearrangement of its internal molecular or microscopic structure, in which atoms are being
L42@P1: moved to new equilibrium positions. This plasticity requires a mechanism for molecular mobility, which in crystalline materials can arise from dislocation motion (discussed further in a
L43@P1: later module.) Materials lacking this mobility, for instance by having internal microstructures
L44@P1: that block dislocation motion, are usually brittle rather than ductile. The stress-strain curve
L45@P1: for brittle materials are typically linear over their full range of strain, eventually terminating in
L46@P1: fracture without appreciable plastic flow.
L47@P1: Note in Fig. 2 that the stress needed to increase the strain beyond the proportional limit
L48@P1: in a ductile material continues to rise beyond the proportional limit; the material requires an
L49@P1: ever-increasing stress to continue straining, a mechanism termed strain hardening.
L50@P1: These microstructural rearrangements associated with plastic flow are usually not reversed
L51@P1-2: 2when the load is removed, so the proportional limit is often the same as or at least close to the
L52@P2: materials’s elastic limit. Elasticity is the property of complete and immediate recovery from
L53@P2: an imposed displacement on release of the load, and the elastic limit is the value of stress at
L54@P2: which the material experiences a permanent residual strain that is not lost on unloading. The
L55@P2: residual strain induced by a given stress can be determined by drawing an unloading line from
L56@P2: the highest point reached on the se - ee curve at that stress back to the strain axis, drawn with
L57@P2: a slope equal to that of the initial elastic loading line. This is done because the material unloads
L58@P2: elastically, there being no force driving the molecular structure back to its original position.
L59@P2: A closely related term is the yield stress, denoted σY in these modules; this is the stress
L60@P2: needed to induce plastic deformation in the specimen. Since it is often difficult to pinpoint the
L61@P2: exact stress at which plastic deformation begins, the yield stress is often taken to be the stress
L62@P2: needed to induce a specified amount of permanent strain, typically 0.2%. The construction used
L63@P2: to find this “offset yield stress” is shown in Fig. 2, in which a line of slope E is drawn from the
L64@P2: strain axis at e =0.2%; this is the unloading line that would result in the specified permanent
L65@P2: strain. The stress at the point of intersection with the σe − e curve is the offset yield stress.
L66@P2: Figure 3 shows the engineering stress-strain curve for copper with an enlarged scale, now
L67@P2: showing strains from zero up to specimen fracture. Here it appears that the rate of strain
L68@P2: hardening2 diminishes up to a point labeled UTS, for Ultimate Tensile Strength (denoted σf in
L69@P2: these modules). Beyond that point, the material appears to strain soften, so that each increment
L70@P2: of additional strain requires a smaller stress.
L71@P2: Figure 3: Full engineering stress-strain curve for annealed polycrystalline copper.
L72@P2: The apparent change from strain hardening to strain softening is an artifact of the plotting
L73@P2: procedure, however, as is the maximum observed in the curve at the UTS. Beyond the yield
L74@P2: point, molecular flow causes a substantial reduction in the specimen cross-sectional area A, so
L75@P2: the true stress σt = P/A actually borne by the material is larger than the engineering stress
L76@P2: computed from the original cross-sectional area (σe = P/A0). The load must equal the true
L77@P2: stress times the actual area (P = σtA), and as long as strain hardening can increase σt enough
L78@P2: to compensate for the reduced area A, the load and therefore the engineering stress will continue
L79@P2: to rise as the strain increases. Eventually, however, the decrease in area due to flow becomes
L80@P2: larger than the increase in true stress due to strain hardening, and the load begins to fall. This

L82@P2-3: 3is a geometrical effect, and if the true stress rather than the engineering stress were plotted no
L83@P3: maximum would be observed in the curve.
L84@P3: At the UTS the differential of the load P is zero, giving an analytical relation between the
L85@P3: true stress and the area at necking:
L86@P3: dA dσt P = σtA → dP =0= σtdA + Adσt →− = (3) A σt
L87@P3: The last expression states that the load and therefore the engineering stress will reach a maximum as a function of strain when the fractional decrease in area becomes equal to the fractional
L88@P3: increase in true stress.
L89@P3: Even though the UTS is perhaps the materials property most commonly reported in tensile
L90@P3: tests, it is not a direct measure of the material due to the influence of geometry as discussed
L91@P3: above, and should be used with caution. The yield stress σY is usually preferred to the UTS in
L92@P3: designing with ductile metals, although the UTS is a valid design criterion for brittle materials
L93@P3: that do not exhibit these flow-induced reductions in cross-sectional area.
L94@P3: The true stress is not quite uniform throughout the specimen, and there will always be
L95@P3: some location - perhaps a nick or some other defect at the surface - where the local stress is
L96@P3: maximum. Once the maximum in the engineering curve has been reached, the localized flow at
L97@P3: this site cannot be compensated by further strain hardening, so the area there is reduced further.
L98@P3: This increases the local stress even more, which accelerates the flow further. This localized and
L99@P3: increasing flow soon leads to a “neck” in the gage length of the specimen such as that seen in
L100@P3: Fig. 4.
L101@P3: Figure 4: Necking in a tensile specimen.
L102@P3: Until the neck forms, the deformation is essentially uniform throughout the specimen, but
L103@P3: after necking all subsequent deformation takes place in the neck. The neck becomes smaller and
L104@P3: smaller, local true stress increasing all the time, until the specimen fails. This will be the failure
L105@P3: mode for most ductile metals. As the neck shrinks, the nonuniform geometry there alters the
L106@P3: uniaxial stress state to a complex one involving shear components as well as normal stresses.
L107@P3: The specimen often fails finally with a “cup and cone” geometry as seen in Fig. 5, in which
L108@P3: the outer regions fail in shear and the interior in tension. When the specimen fractures, the
L109@P3: engineering strain at break — denoted f — will include the deformation in the necked region
L110@P3: and the unnecked region together. Since the true strain in the neck is larger than that in the
L111@P3: unnecked material, the value of f will depend on the fraction of the gage length that has necked.
L112@P3: Therefore, f is a function of the specimen geometry as well as the material, and thus is only a
L113@P3-4: 4crude measure of material ductility.
L114@P4: Figure 5: Cup-and-cone fracture in a ductile metal.

L117@P4: limit followed by a maximum in the curve at which necking takes place. (It is common to term

L178@P7: Among the many possible shapes the true stress-strain curves could assume, let us consider
L179@P7: the concave up, concave down, and sigmoidal shapes shown in Fig. 10. These differ in the
L180@P7: number of tangent points that can be found for the secant line, and produce the following yield
L181@P7: characteristics:
L182@P7: (a) No tangents: Here the curve is always concave upward as in part (a) of Fig. 10, so the
L183@P7: slope of the secant line rises continuously. Therefore the engineering stress rises as well,
L184@P7: without showing a yield drop. Eventually fracture intercedes, so a true stress-strain curve
L185@P7: of this shape identifies a material that fractures before it yields.
L186@P7: (b) One tangent: The curve is concave downward as in part (b) of Fig. 10, so a secant line
L187@P7: reaches a tangent point at λ = λY . The slope of the secant line, and therefore the
L188@P7: engineering stress as well, begins to fall at this point. This is then the yield stress σY seen
L189@P7: as a maximum in stress on a conventional stress-strain curve, and λY is the extension ratio
L190@P7: at yield. The yielding process begins at some adventitious location in the gage length of
L191@P7: the specimen, and continues at that location rather than being initiated elsewhere because
L192@P7: the secant modulus has been reduced at the first location. The specimen is now flowing at
L193@P7: a single location with decreasing resistance, leading eventually to failure. Ductile metals
L194@P7: such as aluminum fail in this way, showing a marked reduction in cross sectional area at
L195@P7: the position of yield and eventual fracture.
L196@P7: (c) Two tangents: For sigmoidal stress-strain curves as in part (c) of Fig. 10, the engineering
L197@P7: stress begins to fall at an extension ration λY , but then rises again at λd. As in the previous
L198@P7: one-tangent case, material begins to yield at a single position when λ = λY , producing
L199@P7: a neck that in turn implies a nonuniform distribution of strain along the gage length.
L200@P7: Material at the neck location then stretches to λd, after which the engineering stress there
L201@P7: would have to rise to stretch it further. But this stress is greater than that needed to
L202@P7: stretch material at the edge of the neck from λY to λd, so material already in the neck
L203@P7: stops stretching and the neck propagates outward from the initial yield location. Only
L204@P7: material within the neck shoulders is being stretched during propagation, with material
L205@P7: inside the necked-down region holding constant at λd, the material’s “natural draw ratio,”
L206@P7: and material outside holding at λY . When all the material has been drawn into the necked
L207@P7: region, the stress begins to rise uniformly in the specimen until eventually fracture occurs.
L208@P7: The increase in strain hardening rate needed to sustain the drawing process in semicrystalline polymers arises from a dramatic transformation in the material’s microstructure. These
L209@P7: materials are initially “spherulitic,” containing flat lamellar crystalline plates, perhaps 10 nm
L210@P7-8: 8thick, arranged radially outward in a spherical domain. As the induced strain increases, these
L211@P8: spherulites are first deformed in the straining direction. As the strain increases further, the
L212@P8: spherulites are broken apart and the lamellar fragments rearranged with a dominantly axial
L213@P8: molecular orientation to become what is known as the fibrillar microstructure. With the strong
L214@P8: covalent bonds now dominantly lined up in the load-bearing direction, the material exhibits
L215@P8: markedly greater strengths and stiffnesses — by perhaps an order of magnitude — than in the
L216@P8: original material. This structure requires a much higher strain hardening rate for increased
L217@P8: strain, causing the upturn and second tangent in the true stress-strain curve.

L273@P10: Compression
L274@P10: The above discussion is concerned primarily with simple tension, i.e. uniaxial loading that
L275@P10: increases the interatomic spacing. However, as long as the loads are sufficiently small (stresses
L276@P10: less than the proportional limit), in many materials the relations outlined above apply equally
L277@P10: well if loads are placed so as to put the specimen in compression rather than tension. The
L278@P10: expression for deformation and a given load δ = P L/AE applies just as in tension, with negative
L279@P10: values for δ and P indicating compression. Further, the modulus E is the same in tension and
L280@P10: compression to a good approximation, and the stress-strain curve simply extends as a straight
L281@P10: line into the third quadrant as shown in Fig. 15.
L282@P10: Figure 15: Stress-strain curve in tension and compression.
L283@P10: There are some practical difficulties in performing stress-strain tests in compression. If
L284@P10: excessively large loads are mistakenly applied in a tensile test, perhaps by wrong settings on the
L285@P10: testing machine, the specimen simply breaks and the test must be repeated with a new specimen.
L286@P10: But in compression, a mistake can easily damage the load cell or other sensitive components,
L287@P10: since even after specimen failure the loads are not necessarily relieved.
L288@P10: Specimens loaded cyclically so as to alternate between tension and compression can exhibit
L289@P10: hysteresis loops if the loads are high enough to induce plastic flow (stresses above the yield
L290@P10: stress). The enclosed area in the loop seen in Fig. 16 is the strain energy per unit volume
L291@P10: released as heat in each loading cycle. This is the well-known tendency of a wire that is being
L292@P10-11: 11bent back and forth to become quite hot at the region of plastic bending. The temperature of
L293@P11: the specimen will rise according to the magnitude of this internal heat generation and the rate
L294@P11: at which the heat can be removed by conduction within the material and convection from the
L295@P11: specimen surface.
L296@P11: Figure 16: Hysteresis loop.
L297@P11: Specimen failure by cracking is inhibited in compression, since cracks will be closed up rather
L298@P11: than opened by the stress state. A number of important materials are much stronger in compression than in tension for this reason. Concrete, for example, has good compressive strength
L299@P11: and so finds extensive use in construction in which the dominant stresses are compressive. But
L300@P11: it has essentially no strength in tension, as cracks in sidewalks and building foundations attest:
L301@P11: tensile stresses appear as these structures settle, and cracks begin at very low tensile strain in
L302@P11: unreinforced concrete.
