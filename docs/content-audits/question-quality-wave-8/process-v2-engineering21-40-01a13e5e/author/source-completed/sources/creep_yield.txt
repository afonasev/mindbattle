L6@P0: Introduction
L7@P0: Phenomenological treatments such those outlined in the Module on Yield and Plastic Flow
L8@P0: (Module 20) are very useful for engineering predictions, but they provide only limited insight
L9@P0: to the molecular mechanisms underlying yield. Molecular understanding is a higher level of
L10@P0: insight, and also guides processing adjustments that can optimize the material. As discussed in
L11@P0: the Module on Atomistics of Elasticity (Module 2), the high level of order present in crystalline
L12@P0: materials lead to good atomistic models for the stiffness. Early workers naturally sought an
L13@P0: atomistic treatment of the yield process as well. This turned out to be a much more subtle
L14@P0: problem than might have been anticipated, and required hypothesizing a type of crystalline
L15@P0: defect — the “dislocation” — to explain the experimentally observed results. Dislocation theory
L16@P0: permits a valuable intuitive understanding of yielding in crystalline materials, and explains how
L17@P0: yielding can be controlled by alloying and heat treatment. It is one of the principal triumphs of
L18@P0: the last century of materials science.

L56@P1: Edge, screw, and mixed dislocations
L57@P1: A rationale for the apparently low experimental values for the yield strengths of crystalline
L58@P1: materials was proposed independently by Taylor, Polyani and Orowan in 1934. These workers
L59@P1: realized that it was not necessary to slip entire planes of atoms past one another to deform the
L60@P1: material plastically, a process that would require breaking all the bonds connecting the planes
L61@P1: simultaneously. The stress needed to do this would be very high, on the order of G/10 as
L62@P1: described above. But it isn’t necessary to move all the atoms at once; only a few at a time need
L63@P1: to move, requiring a much smaller stress. Analogously to the way an inchworm moves, only those
L64@P1: atoms lying in a plane above a single line might be displaced one atomic spacing. This would
L65@P1: force the plane of atoms previously there into a midway position as shown in Fig. 2, creating an
L66@P1: “extra” plane of atoms halfway between the normal equilibrium positions. The termination of
L67@P1: this plane then constitutes a line defect in the crystal known as a dislocation1.
L68@P1: Viewed end-on as seen in Fig. 3, it can be appreciated that the extra plane of atoms creates
L69@P1: a region of compression near the plane but above the dislocation line, and a tensile region below
L70@P1: it. In a “soft” crystal whose interatomic bonds are relatively compliant, the distortion extends
L71@P1: an appreciable distance from the dislocation. Conversely, in “hard” crystals with stiffer bonds
L72@P1: the distortion is confined to a smaller region near the dislocation. The face-centered cubic (fcc)
L73@P1: metals such as copper and gold have close-packed planes (those with (111) Miller indices), which

L75@P1: The Structure of Materials, John Wiley & Sons, New York, 1999.

L78@P2: bonds, so that the dislocation width is large. The dislocation width is substantially smaller in
L79@P2: the body centered cubic (bcc) metals such as iron and steel, smaller still in ionically bonded
L80@P2: ceramics, and even smaller in covalently bonded ceramics.

L82@P2: The dislocation associated with this extra plane of atoms can be moved easily, since only a
L83@P2: small adjustment in position is required to break the bonds on the next plane over and allow them
L84@P2: to form on the originally “extra” plane. Now the third plane is the extra one, and the dislocation
L85@P2: will have moved by one atomic position. Slip is obviously made much easier if dislocation motion
L86@P2: is available to the material. In fact, it first appears the dislocation concept does too good a job
L87@P2: in explaining crystal plasticity, since the dislocation is in a balanced metastable position and
L88@P2: should be capable of being moved either left or right with a vanishingly small force. If this were
L89@P2: true, the crystal would have essentially no shear strength.
L90@P2: However, as the dislocation moves it drags with it the regions of compressive and tensile
L91@P2: distortion in the lattice around it. This is accompanied by a sort of frictional drag, giving rise to
L92@P2: a resistance to dislocation motion known as the Peierls force. This force is dependent on such
L93@P2: factors as the crystal type and the temperature, and this plays an important role in determining
L94@P2: the material’s yield stress. As seen in Table 1, materials with wide dislocations have low Peierls
L95@P2: forces, since the distortion is spread out over a large volume and is much less intense at its core.

L221@P6: Dislocation motion and hardening
L222@P6: The ductility of crystalline materials is determined by dislocation mobility, and factors that
L223@P6: impede dislocation motion can produce dramatic increases in the material’s yield strength.
L224@P6: This increased resistance to plastic flow also raises the indentation hardness of the material, so
L225@P6: strengthening of this sort is known as hardening. Alloying elements, grain boundaries, and even
L226@P6: dislocations themselves can provide this impediment, and these provide the means by which
L227@P6: the materials technologist controls yield. A thorough treatment of these important concepts
L228@P6: must be left to subjects in physical metallurgy, but the following paragraphs will provide a brief
L229@P6: introduction to some of them.
L230@P6: When one dislocation, moving on its slip plane under the influence of a driving shear stress,
L231@P6: passes through another a “jog” will be created in the second dislocation as shown in Fig. 8.

L233@P7: The portion of the dislocation line in the jog is now no longer on its original glide plane, and
L234@P7: is “pinned” in position. If the dislocation concentration is large, these jogs become a powerful
L235@P7: impediment to plastic flow by dislocation motion. Paradoxically, the very dislocations that
L236@P7: permit plastic flow in the first place can impede it if they become too numerous.
L237@P7: Figure 9: Dislocation bowing.

L319@P10: A related phenomenon accounts for the very high strengths (≈ 4 GPa, or 600 kpsi ) of
L320@P10: piano wire, a eutectoid steel that has been drawn through a sequence of reducing dies to obtain
L321@P10: a small final diameter. The “pearlitic” structure obtained on cooling this steel through the
L322@P10: eutectoid temperature is a two-phase mixture of Fe3C (“cementite”) in bcc iron (“ferrite”). As
L323@P10: the diameter is reduced during drawing, the ferrite cells are reduced as well, forming a structure
L324@P10: analogous to a fine-grained metal. The cell boundaries restrict dislocation motion, leading to
L325@P10: the very high yield strengths.
L326@P10: Impurity atoms in solid solution can also serve to harden a crystalline material by impeding
L327@P10: dislocation motion; this is called solution strengthening. An impurity atom smaller than the
L328@P10: atoms of the host lattice will create an approximately spherical tensile field around itself which
L329@P10: will attract the compressive regions around mobile dislocations, and a larger impurity atom will
L330@P10: tend to trap the tensile region of nearby dislocations. On average, the population of dislocations
L331@P10: will maneuver so as to lower their strain energies by associating with the nonuniform strain fields
L332@P10: around impurities. This association impedes dislocation motion, which inhibits plastic flow and
L333@P10: increases the yield stress.
L334@P10: Solution hardening is not usually an especially effective strengthening mechanism in commercial materials, largely because the solubility of impurity atoms is not sufficient to generate
L335@P10: an appreciable number of obstacles. One important exception to this is the iron-carbon, or
L336@P10: steel, system. If steel at approximately the eutectoid carbon composition (0.8% C) is cooled
L337@P10: rapidly from above the eutectoid temperature of 723◦C, the carbon atoms can become trapped
L338@P10: in the iron lattice at much higher concentrations than bcc iron’s equilibrium carbon solubility
L339@P10: would normally allow. (This tendency for trapping can be enhanced by alloying elements such
L340@P10: as chromium and molybdenum, which have an affinity for carbon and thus reduce its ability to
L341@P10: diffuse away.) To accommodate these metastable impurity atoms, the iron lattice transforms
L342@P10: to a body-centered tetragonal form named martensite (see Fig. 14), with a strong nonspherical
L343@P10: strain field around the carbon atoms. These tetragonal distortions are very effective impediments to dislocation motion, making martensite an extremely hard phase. The periodic water
L344@P10: quenches a blacksmith uses during metalworking is done (perhaps without the smith knowing
L345@P10: why it works) to tailor the material’s hardness by developing martensitic inclusions in the steel.
L346@P10: Martensite is so hard and brittle that the rapidly quenched steel must usually be tempered by
L347@P10: heating it to approximately 400◦C for an hour or so. This allows diffusion of carbon to take place,
L348@P10: creating a dispersion of cementite inclusions; it also permits recovery of the dislocations present
L349@P10: in the martensite. The resulting material is much tougher than the as-formed martensitic steel,
L350@P10: but still retains a high strength level due to the strengthening effect of the carbide inclusions.

L353@P11: Kinetics of creep in crystalline materials
L354@P11: “Creep” is the term used to describe the tendency of many materials to exhibit continuing
L355@P11: deformation even though the stress is held constant. Viscoelastic polymers exhibit creep, as
L356@P11: was discussed in Module 19. However, creep also occurs in polycrystalline metallic and ceramic
L357@P11: systems, most importantly when the the temperature is higher than approximately half their
