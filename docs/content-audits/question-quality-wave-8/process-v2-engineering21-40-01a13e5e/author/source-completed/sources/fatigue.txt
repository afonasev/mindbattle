L6@P0: Introduction
L7@P0: The concept of \fatigue" arose several times in the Module on Fracture (Module 23), as in the
L8@P0: growth of cracks in the Comet aircraft that led to disaster when they became large enough
L9@P0: to propagate catastrophically as predicted by the Griffith criterion. Fatigue, as understood
L10@P0: by materials technologists, is a process in which damage accumulates due to the repetitive
L11@P0: application of loads that may be well below the yield point. The process is dangerous because a
L12@P0: single application of the load would not produce any ill effects, and a conventional stress analysis
L13@P0: might lead to a assumption of safety that does not exist.
L14@P0: In one popular view of fatigue in metals, the fatigue process is thought to begin at an
L15@P0: internal or surface f aw where the stresses are concentrated, and consists initially of shear f ow
L16@P0: along slip planes. Over a number of cycles, this slip generates intrusions and extrusions that
L17@P0: begin to resemble a crack. A true crack running inward from an intrusion region may propagate
L18@P0: initially along one of the original slip planes, but eventually turns to propagate transversely to
L19@P0: the principal normal stress as seen in Fig. 1.
L20@P0: Figure 1: Intrusion-extrusion model of fatigue crack initiation.
L21@P0: When the failure surface of a fatigued specimen is examined, a region of slow crack growth
L22@P0: is usually evident in the form of a \clamshell" concentric around the location of the initial f aw.
L23@P0: (See Fig. 2.) The clamshell region often contains concentric \beach marks" at which the crack
L24@P0: was arrested for some number of cycles before resuming its growth. Eventually, the crack may
L25@P0: become large enough to satisfy the energy or stress intensity criteria for rapid propagation,
L26@P0: following the previous expressions for fracture mechanics. This final phase produces the rough
L27@P0: surface typical of fast fracture. In postmortem examination of failed parts, it is often possible to

L31@P1: at failure from the size of the crack just before rapid propagation and the fracture toughness of
L32@P1: the material.

L35@P1: The modern study of fatigue is generally dated from the work of A. Wohler, a technologist in
L36@P1: the German railroad system in the mid-nineteenth century. Wohler was concerned by the failure
L37@P1: of axles after various times in service, at loads considerably less than expected. A railcar axle
L38@P1: is essentially a round beam in four-point bending, which produces a compressive stress along
L39@P1: the top surface and a tensile stress along the bottom (see Fig. 3). After the axle has rotated a
L40@P1: half turn, the bottom becomes the top and vice versa, so the stresses on a particular region of
L41@P1: material at the surface varies sinusoidally from tension to compression and back again. This is
L42@P1: now known as fully reversed fatigue loading.
L43@P1: Figure 3: Fatigue in a railcar axle.

L46@P2: Well before a microstructural understanding of fatigue processes was developed, engineers had
L47@P2: developed empirical means of quantifying the fatigue process and designing against it. Perhaps
L48@P2: the most important concept is the S-N diagram, such as those shown in Fig. 41, in which a
L49@P2: constant cyclic stress amplitude  is applied to a specimen and the number of loading cycles 
L50@P2: until the specimen fails is determined. Millions of cycles might be required to cause failure at
L51@P2: lower loading levels, so the abscissa in usually plotted logarithmically.
L52@P2: Figure 4:  −  curves for aluminum and low-carbon steel.
L53@P2: In some materials, notably ferrous alloys, the  −  curve f attens out eventually, so that
L54@P2: below a certain endurance limit σ failure does not occur no matter how long the loads are
L55@P2: cycled. Obviously, the designer will size the structure to keep the stresses below σ by a suitable
L56@P2: safety factor if cyclic loads are to be withstood. For some other materials such as aluminum, no
L57@P2: endurance limit exists and the designer must arrange for the planned lifetime of the structure
L58@P2: to be less than the failure point on the  −  curve.
L59@P2: Statistical variability is troublesome in fatigue testing; it is necessary to measure the lifetimes
L60@P2: of perhaps twenty specimens at each of ten or so load levels to define the  −  curve with
L61@P2: statistical confidence2. It is generally impossible to cycle the specimen at more than approximately 10Hz (inertia in components of the testing machine and heating of the specimen often
L62@P2: become problematic at higher speeds) and at that speed it takes 11.6 days to reach 107 cycles
L63@P2: of loading. Obtaining a full  −  curve is obviously a tedious and expensive procedure.

L68@P2-3: flAt first glance, the scatter in measured lifetimes seems enormous, especially given the logarithmic scale of the abscissa. If the coefficient of variability in conventional tensile testing is
L69@P3: usually only a few percent, why do the fatigue lifetimes vary over orders of magnitude? It must
L70@P3: be remembered that in tensile testing, we are measuring the variability in stress at a given
L71@P3: number of cycles (one), while in fatigue we are measuring the variability in cycles at a given
L72@P3: stress. Stated differently, in tensile testing we are generating vertical scatter bars, but in fatigue
L73@P3: they are horizontal (see Fig. 5). Note that we must expect more variability in the lifetimes as
L74@P3: the  −  curve becomes f atter, so that materials that are less prone to fatigue damage require
L75@P3: more specimens to provide a given confidence limit on lifetime.

L79@P3: shown in Fig. 6. Such a cycle can be phrased in several ways, a common one being to state the
L80@P3: alternating stress σ and the stress ratio  = σ/σ. For fully reversed loading,  = −1.

L84@P3: A very substantial amount of testing is required to obtain an  −  curve for the simple
L85@P3: case of fully reversed loading, and it will usually be impractical to determine whole families of
L86@P3: curves for every combination of mean and alternating stress. There are a number of strategems
L87@P3: for finessing this difficulty, one common one being the Goodman diagram. shown in Fig. 7. Here
L88@P3: a graph is constructed with mean stress as the abscissa and alternating stress as the ordinate,
L89@P3: and a straight \lifeline" is drawn from σ on the σ axis to the ultimate tensile stress σ on the
L90@P3: σ axis. Then for any given mean stress, the endurance limit | the value of alternating stress
L91@P3: at which fatigue fracture never occurs | can be read directly as the ordinate of the lifeline at
L92@P3: that value of σ. Alternatively, if the design application dictates a given ratio of σ to σ,a
L93@P3: line is drawn from the origin with a slope equal to that ratio. Its intersection with the lifeline
L94@P3: then gives the effective endurance limit for that combination of σ and σ.

L129@P5: Miner's \law" should be viewed like many other material \laws," a useful approximation,
L130@P5: quite easy to apply, that might be accurate enough to use in design. But damage accumulation
L131@P5: in fatigue is usually a complicated mixture of several different mechanisms, and the assumption
L132@P5: of linear damage accumulation inherent in Miner's law should be viewed skeptically. If portions
L133@P5: of the material's microstructure become unable to bear load as fatigue progresses, the stress
L134@P5: must be carried by the surviving microstructural elements. The rate of damage accumulation
L135@P5: in these elements then increases, so that the material suffers damage much more rapidly in
L136@P5: the last portions of its fatigue lifetime. If on the other hand cyclic loads induce strengthening
L137@P5: mechanisms such as molecular orientation or crack blunting, the rate of damage accumulation
L138@P5: could drop during some part of the material's lifetime. Miner's law ignores such effects, and
L139@P5: often fails to capture the essential physics of the fatigue process.
